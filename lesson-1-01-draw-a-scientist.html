📘 The Lesson: Exploring Science
What do scientists do — and how do they record it?
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 — What Is a Scientist?

Here's the truth: anyone can be a scientist. A scientistScientistA person who studies the natural world by making careful observations, asking questions, and investigating. is simply someone who looks carefully at the world, asks questions, and tries to find answers. Scientists work in forests, in oceans, in hospitals, in kitchens, in outer space — and yes, in laboratories wearing white coats too!

🤔
Stop & Think
"Can you think of a time when YOU acted like a scientist? Maybe you wondered why something happened, or looked closely at a bug, or tried something different to see what would happen?"

Part 2 — The Scientific Sketch

One of the most important tools a scientist has isn't fancy equipment — it's a scientific sketchScientific SketchA careful, detailed drawing that scientists use to record and communicate what they observe.. A scientific sketch is a detailed drawing that records what you observeObservationUsing your senses to carefully notice and record details about something in the world around you.. Along with the drawing, scientists add labelsLabelA word or short phrase written on a drawing to identify and name its different parts., questions, and explanations.

You don't have to be an artist to create a great scientific sketch. You simply have to take your time, look closely, and record what you see!

📋 The 5 Rules of Scientific Sketching
A
Accurate — Draw exactly what you see. No guessing or imagining extra details!
B
Big — Use the entire page! A big drawing is easier to see and add details to.
C
Color — Add color to your drawing. Real colors help record what you truly observed.
D
Details — Focus on the small things! Texture, patterns, edges, and features make your sketch scientific.
E
Explanations / Labels — Write labels and notes that explain what you drew. A drawing with words is even more powerful!
👆

Tap on any part of the sample sketch to see which of the 5 rules it follows!

🔑 Key Concept

A scientific sketchScientific SketchA careful, detailed drawing that scientists use to record and communicate what they observe. is a scientist's superpower. It captures details that words alone can't describe. When you add labelsLabelA word or short phrase written on a drawing to identify and name its different parts., your drawing becomes a diagramDiagramA drawing that shows how something works or what its different parts are and how they connect. — a powerful communication tool that scientists share with each other!

Remember: it's not about being a good artist. It's about being a careful observer.

🤔
Stop & Reason
"Why do scientists use sketches instead of just writing words? What can a drawing communicate that a sentence can't?"

Part 3 — Scientists Come in All Kinds!

One of the biggest surprises students discover: scientists don't look one particular way! Scientists work in all kinds of settings, and they look like everyone.

Field Scientist
Works outdoors — in forests, oceans, mountains, and jungles — observing nature up close.
Lab Scientist
Works indoors in a laboratory with equipment like microscopes, beakers, and computers.
Citizen Scientist
Regular people — even kids like you — who observe and record data to help real scientists!
🌍

Real World Connection: Today, thousands of students around the world contribute to real science! Apps like iNaturalist let anyone upload nature observations that professional scientists use in their research. You are already a scientist!

Curious? Go Deeper — Famous Scientists and Their Notebooks

Throughout history, the greatest scientists kept detailed notebooks full of sketches. Charles Darwin sketched animals he observed on the Galápagos Islands — his drawings helped him develop the theory of evolution. Marie Curie kept careful laboratory notebooks (they're still mildly radioactive today — scientists wear gloves to read them!). Nikola Tesla sketched his electrical inventions before building them. All of these scientists understood: if you draw it, you understand it better.

Your science notebook this year is part of that same tradition. Treat it like a scientist would — with care, curiosity, and detail!

🔬 Guided Exploration: How to Make a Scientific Sketch
Follow the 4 steps of a real scientist!
⏱ 8 min

Let's trace the steps a scientist follows every time they make a scientific sketch. Follow along as we work through each step!

1

👁️ Observe First — Before you draw a single line, spend 30 seconds just looking. What shapes do you notice? What colors? Are there patterns? What makes this thing unique? ObservationObservationUsing your senses to carefully notice and record details about something in the world around you. is the most important step. Scientists say: "Observe before you draw."

2

📏 Start Big — Draw the main outline first. Make it fill most of the page! Don't start tiny — you can always add, but you can't add space you didn't leave. Scientists use light pencil lines for their first outline so they can adjust easily.

3

🔍 Add Details & Color — Now zoom in! Add the small lines, textures, and features that make your subject unique. Then add realistic color. Remember: accuracyAccuracyDrawing exactly what you see, with careful attention to real shapes, sizes, and details. means using the colors you actually see, not what you think it should be!

4

🏷️ Label & Explain — Write labelsLabelA word or short phrase written on a drawing to identify and name its different parts. with lines pointing to important parts. Add the date, location, and a sentence of explanation. Your drawing is now a scientific diagramDiagramA drawing that shows how something works or what its different parts are and how they connect.!

🤔
Think Like a Scientist
"Why do you think scientists spend time observing before they draw? What might you miss if you jumped straight into drawing without looking first?"
🤯
Mind-Blowing Fact
When researchers showed children a picture of a scientist and asked "Does this look like a scientist?" more than 70% said NO — because the person was a woman, or a person of color, or young. But in reality, scientists are every age, gender, and background. The most important thing a scientist needs is curiosity!
Curious? Go Deeper — What the Research Says

Scientists who study how children see science have been running an experiment called the "Draw a Scientist" test since the 1950s. They ask students to draw a scientist — and then they look for patterns in what students draw.

Over 70 years ago, almost every student drew a white man with wild hair in a lab coat. Today, thanks to seeing more diverse scientists in books, TV, and school, students are starting to draw scientists that look much more like the real scientific community — diverse, young, old, and working everywhere from oceans to space stations.

Your "Draw a Scientist" assignment today is part of this same tradition — and your drawing tells us something about what you believe about science!

New Scientist Tool — You'll Use This All Year!
Meet C·E·R

Real scientists don't just say what they think — they back it up with evidence and explain why the evidence matters. That's what CER helps you do. Every time you write a CER response, you're thinking and writing like a real scientist.

C
Claim
Your answer in one clear sentence. What do you think is true?
E
Evidence
A specific thing you saw, learned, or observed that proves your claim.
R
Reasoning
How does your evidence connect to your claim? Why does it prove it?
Example — "Why do scientists use notebooks?"

C: Scientists use notebooks because they need to record their observations accurately. E: In the lesson, I learned that a scientific sketch includes labels, details, and explanations — not just pictures. R: This makes sense because if you only draw and don't write notes, you might forget important details later — and science needs to be repeatable.
