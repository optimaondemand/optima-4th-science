📘 The Lesson: Three Types of Rock
Discover what makes each rock type unique!
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 — What Are Rocks Made Of?

Rocks are all around us — they make up mountains, cliffs, riverbeds, and even the ground beneath your feet. But did you know that not all rocks are the same? Scientists called geologists have discovered that there are three main types of rocks, and each type forms in a completely different way.

Before we look at the types, let's ask a big question:

🤔
Stop & Think
"All rocks are made of minerals — natural solid materials like quartz or feldspar. But if they're all made of minerals, why do rocks look so different from each other? What might explain the differences?"

💡 Use evidence! What does the formation process of each rock type tell you about why igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks look so different?

The answer comes down to how each rock formed. Whether rock melted, was pressed into layers, or was cooked by heat deep underground — that's what determines the type. Let's explore each one!

👆

Tap on any part of the diagram — the water, the rock layers, or the magma chamber — to learn how each forms.

🔑 Key Concept

The same minerals can form very different rocks depending on conditions. Limestone (sedimentary) heated under pressure becomes marble (metamorphic). Marble melted deep underground becomes magma, which cools into igneous rock. It's all connected!

Think of it like butter, toast, and croutons: same starting material, totally different results depending on what you do to it.

🤔
Stop & Reason
"Metamorphic rocks form from heat and pressure — but the rock doesn't melt. Why do you think it matters that the rock changes without melting? How would the result be different if it melted?"

Part 2 — The Three Rock Types Up Close

Igneous
Born from fire! Forms when melted rock cools and hardens. Examples: granite, basalt, obsidian.
Sedimentary
Built in layers! Forms from pressed sediment over millions of years. Often has fossils! Examples: sandstone, limestone, shale.
Metamorphic
Changed by nature! Forms when rock transforms under intense heat and pressure. Examples: marble, slate.

🌍

Real-World Connection: The white marble used in sculptures and famous buildings like the Parthenon in Greece? That's metamorphic rock — it used to be limestone! The black countertops in many kitchens are often granite — an igneous rock formed miles underground. Rock is everywhere once you know how to look!

⛏️ Resources Stored in Rocks

Rocks aren't just interesting to look at — they hold valuable natural resources that people depend on every day! Coal (a sedimentary rock) stores energy we burn for electricity. Iron ore hidden inside rocks is melted down to make steel for buildings and cars. Even the sand and gravel in concrete come from crushed rock. Oil and natural gas are trapped deep inside layers of sedimentary rock underground. When scientists study rocks, they're also studying where Earth stores the resources that power our world.

Crystal Size Tells the Story: How Fast Did the Lava Cool?

In igneous rock, crystal size tells you how fast the lava cooled. Fast cooling = tiny crystals. Slow cooling deep underground = big sparkly crystals you can see!
Curious? Go Deeper — The Secret Life of Minerals

Minerals are the building blocks of all rocks. Every rock you pick up is made of one or more minerals — natural solid materials with a specific chemical recipe. Different combinations of minerals give rocks their unique colors, textures, and properties.

Granite, for example, contains three main minerals: quartz (usually clear or white, and very hard), feldspar (often pink or white, the most common mineral in Earth's crust), and mica (shiny silver or gold flakes that catch the light). That's why granite often looks speckled!

Scientists use a tool called the Mohs Hardness Scale to rank minerals from 1 (softest — talc, like baby powder) to 10 (hardest — diamond). A simple field test: if your fingernail scratches a mineral, it's very soft. If it takes a steel key, it's harder. If nothing scratches it, it might be quartz or even diamond!

🔬 Guided Exploration: The Rock Cycle
Follow a rock's incredible journey through time!
⏱ 8 min

Rocks don't stay the same forever. Given enough time and the right conditions, any rock can transform into any other type. This ongoing journey is called the rock cycleRock CycleThe ongoing process by which rocks slowly change from one type to another over millions of years.. Let's follow the path step by step!

1

Weathering & Erosion: Rocks at Earth's surface are slowly broken apart by wind, water, ice, and even plant roots into tiny pieces called sedimentSedimentTiny pieces of rock, sand, mud, or shell that settle in layers at the bottom of water bodies.. This can take thousands of years — but the process never stops!

2

Sedimentation & Compaction: Rivers and streams carry sedimentSedimentTiny pieces of rock, sand, mud, or shell that settle in layers. and drop it in layers at the bottom of lakes and oceans. Over millions of years, the layers pile up and press together so tightly they cement into sedimentary rockSedimentaryRock that forms from layers of sediment pressed and cemented together..

3

Heat & Pressure — Going Deeper: When rock gets buried even further underground, the intense heat and crushing pressure transform it into metamorphic rockMetamorphicRock formed when existing rock is changed by heat and pressure deep underground. — without melting! The minerals rearrange into new patterns, creating wavy bands or a completely new texture.

4

Melting & Cooling — Back to Igneous: Push rock even deeper and eventually it melts into magma. When that magma cools — slowly underground as graniteIgneousRock formed when magma or lava cools and hardens., or quickly on the surface as basalt or obsidian — the cycle begins again! A rock that's billions of years old could be on its seventh or eighth trip around the cycle.

🤔
Think Like a Scientist
"The rock cycle has no beginning and no end — it's been going on for billions of years. If you picked up a piece of granite today, that rock might have once been sedimentary rock, and before that, a different igneous rock. How does that make you think differently about the rocks around you?"
🤯
Mind-Blowing Fact
Diamonds are a type of mineralMineralA natural, non-living solid material that makes up rocks. that forms about 100 miles underground under crushing pressure and temperatures over 2,000°F. They travel to Earth's surface tucked inside special volcanic eruptions — and they've been growing in the ground for over a billion years!
Curious? Go Deeper — Fossils: Nature's Time Capsules

One of the most exciting things about sedimentary rock is that it can preserve fossils — the remains or imprints of plants and animals that lived long ago. When an organism dies near a body of water, sediment can quickly cover it before it decays. Over millions of years, the sediment hardens into rock, and the organism's shape becomes locked inside forever.

Fossils only form in sedimentary rock. If a sedimentary rock gets turned into metamorphic rock by heat and pressure, any fossils inside are destroyed. If it melts into magma, they're completely gone. That's why fossils are so rare — the conditions have to be just right!

The Grand Canyon in Arizona exposes nearly two billion years of sedimentary rock layers. Walking down into the canyon is like traveling back in time — the deeper you go, the older the rock. Geologists read these layers like pages in a history book, and the fossils they find tell us which creatures lived in each era.
