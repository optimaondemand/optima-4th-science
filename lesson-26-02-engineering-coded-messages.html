📘 The Lesson: Patterns Carry Messages
Engineers design codes to send information
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Patterns That Mean Something

A patternPatternA repeating arrangement with meaning. is something that repeats in a way you can recognize, like three slow knocks or two short flashes. When a pattern means something, like “open the door,” it becomes a codeCodeRules for turning ideas into patterns..

Codes are how we transmitTransmitSend information. messages with waves of light, sound or radio. Phone signals use patterns of radio waves. Even traffic lights are a simple code: red, yellow and green each mean something.

Try it! Send a message with a flashlight key, the way Morse code does.

Part 2 · Designing a Code

Real engineers start a design with criteriaCriteriaWhat success looks like. (the goals) and constraintsConstraintThe limits. (what you can't do). For a coded message system, you might say:

  • Criteria: can send the message "YES" or "NO." Partner can decode in under 1 minute.
  • Constraints: use only a flashlight (no talking). Must work from 10 feet away.
  • RedesignRedesignImprove after testing.: if they decoded it wrong, find what was confusing and change it.

The most famous coded system in history was Morse code. In the 1800s, telegraph operators tapped Morse code over wires, and railroads used it to run trains safely. Later, ships sent it by radio and flashed it with lamps.

Watch out: some people think a code has to be complicated to work. Simple codes are often best: fewer parts to remember, fewer ways to misread.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

What is a CODE?

AA computer program
BA set of rules that turns ideas into patterns and back again
CA secret you can't share with anyone
🔤 Right! A code maps ideas to patterns. Morse code maps letters to dots and dashes. Sign language maps words to handshapes. Even traffic lights are a code.
A code is broader than just programming. It's any system of rules that turns IDEAS into PATTERNS and lets a receiver decode them. Morse code, sign language, and emojis are all codes.
Q2

You're designing a flashlight code. Your partner can't tell ONE flash from TWO short flashes — they keep mis-counting. Which engineering step do you do next?

ARedesign — make the flashes more clearly different
BDecide the code can't be improved
CUse a totally different code that's also confusing
🔧 Right! Redesign is what you DO with a failure. Make the patterns more clearly different (longer pauses, mix in long flashes) so your partner can decode reliably.
Failures during testing are GOOD — they show you what to fix. Engineers don't give up; they redesign. Make the patterns easier to tell apart.
Q3

Which is an example of a CRITERION (a goal) for a knock-code design?

A"No talking allowed"
B"Only one hand"
C"Partner must decode 3 different messages correctly"
✅ Right! A criterion is a GOAL — what the design needs to do. "Decode 3 messages correctly" is the goal. "No talking" is a constraint (a limit).
"No talking" and "only one hand" are CONSTRAINTS (limits on what you can do). Criteria are GOALS — like "decode 3 messages correctly" or "work from 20 feet away."
