About This Unit

This unit starts with one big idea, that almost all the energy in living things comes from the Sun, and builds from there: food chains and food webs, how an ecosystem stays healthy, how body parts help living things survive, how animals sense the world, how traits are passed down, and how life cycles and seasons repeat.

Almost every example is from Florida: the Everglades, pine forests, springs and beaches. The last week turns to the resources people use, like water, sunlight, oil and phosphate, and where they’re found in Florida.

By the end, your student should be able to trace energy from the Sun to an animal, explain how a body part helps a living thing survive, name the four steps an animal uses to react, and back up a claim with evidence.

“The hawk never ate a plant, but every bite it took held energy a plant caught from the Sun.”The big idea that runs through Unit 3, from the case in Lesson 9.01

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons on one topic. The first usually explains an idea; the second often has your student build a model (a labeled drawing that explains how something works) or work with real data. Each lesson follows the same path:

📚 VocabularyA quick warm-up and the day’s six words
📘 The LessonReading, pictures and short “Stop & Think” questions
🔬 ExploreA step-by-step activity in the notebook
🧠 Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
✍️ AssignmentShow what you know, then turn it in
  • ❓A Big Question at the top that the lesson answers by the end
  • 🔤Part A: four vocabulary blanks from a word bank
  • ✏️Part B: three short answers in complete sentences
  • 🎨Part C: a drawing, chart or model in the notebook
  • ⚖️Use Your Evidence: agree or disagree with a claim, with two pieces of evidence
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “Plants don’t really need sunlight; they get their food from the soil,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

The most common slip is giving an opinion with no evidence. A helpful question at home: “What in the lesson shows that?”

New: The Field Station (Lessons 9.01 to 15.02)

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition. A field scientist, Dr. Lena Reyes, checks in over a walkie-talkie, and each lesson has a mystery to solve. In 9.01, a hawk’s pellet (a lump of fur and bones it coughs up) shows it eats mice, frogs and bugs, never plants. So how does it run on sunlight? In 9.02, the frogs leave the same marsh after a dry spell, and a volunteer predicts the hawk will starve. Is she right? In 10.01, a giant live oak falls in a storm and the whole forest around it changes. Why? In 10.02, airplane counts of manatees jump by 1,275 in a single year. Did that many really show up? In 11.01, a rain-soaked notebook holds four photos of a mystery bird. How does it find food it can’t see? In 11.02, a refuge needs a sign, and only one of three drawings really explains the bird. In 12.01, four trail cameras catch four Florida animals reacting at almost the same moment. Why? In 12.02, a camera by a pond sees moths vanish in the dark, but a bat detector hears clicks. What caught them? In 13.01, seeds from one wild beach sunflower grow into two very different plants. Did the seeds get mixed up? In 13.02, a sunny garden full of flowers makes almost no seeds. Where did they go? In 14.01, dozens of little brown bug shells cling to the cattails at a pond, and a green case hangs on a milkweed. Dead bugs? In 14.02, it’s January: the pond is full of ducks and robins, but postcards from Maine show a frozen, quiet pond. Does Florida even have seasons? In 15.01, satellite photos show square lakes popping up across central Florida. What is making them, and will what’s being taken ever come back? In 15.02, a volunteer named Marco pins four spots on a Florida map where he wants to dig for phosphate. Will any of them work?

The Unit, Week by Week

Each week shows both lessons, the words your student will use, and two easy ways to keep the science going at home.

9Week
Every animal runs on energy that started in the Sun.Energy from the Sun Field Station · new
Lesson 9.01Energy Pathways: From Sun to Life“How does the energy from the Sun end up inside a gopher tortoise, a hawk, or you?”Solves a mystery: a red-shouldered hawk in the Everglades never eats a plant, so how can it run on sunlight? Traces their own lunch back to the Sun, runs a light test (grass in a sunny window vs. a dark closet), finds what sawgrass needs on a real marsh photo, and builds the hawk’s food chain: Sun → sawgrass → grasshopper → green tree frog → hawk.Words: energy, producer, consumer, photosynthesis, food chain, decomposer
Lesson 9.02The Power of Producers and Consumers“What happens to the rest of the food chain if one living thing disappears?”Solves part 2 of the hawk mystery: the green tree frogs leave the marsh, and a volunteer predicts the hawk will starve and nothing else will change. Sorts quick “more or fewer?” predictions, finds a real photo of a red-shouldered hawk eating a snake, runs a missing-link model (remove the frogs or the hawks and watch four seasons), and stamps each of the volunteer’s predictions supported or not supported.Words: food web, predator
Ask at dinner
“If all the grasshoppers disappeared, who would go hungry? Who might have too much to eat?”
Try at home
Pick something from dinner and trace it back to the Sun together. (Chicken → corn the chicken ate → Sun.) Or put one plant in a dark closet and one in a sunny window for a week, and compare.
10Week
Living things depend on each other in more ways than eating.A web of connections Field Station · new
Lesson 10.01Web of Life“What other ways are living things in a Florida forest connected, besides eating each other?”Solves a mystery: a giant live oak falls, and the owl, the squirrels and even the bears nearby are affected. Ties the oak to its neighbors, “pulls threads” in a web to predict what changes, and sorts a saw palmetto’s five kinds of connections: food, pollination, shelter, nutrients and human care.Words: ecosystem, habitat, pollination, pollinator, nutrients, web of life
Lesson 10.02Ecosystem Balance“How can we tell if a Florida ecosystem is healthy, and what can we do when it’s not?”Solves a second mystery: manatee counts from airplanes jumped by 1,275 in one year. Flies a practice survey to see how weather changes a count, stamps four Florida stories (manatees, pythons, lionfish, wood storks) as recovering or out of balance, reads 30 years of real survey flights, and builds a manatee line graph.Words: native species, invasive species, population, balance, conservation, human impact
Ask at dinner
“If one big tree fell in a forest, who would miss it, and why?” Then, after 10.02: “How do scientists know a change is real and not just a guess?” (Listen for: they collect numbers for a long time.)
Try at home
Look at one tree near home. Count the living things that use it: birds, bugs, lizards, moss. For 10.02: count the birds you see from one window for 5 minutes, two days in a row. Did the birds change, or did the weather?
11Week
Every body part has a job.Built to survive Field Station · new
Lesson 11.01Inside Out: Plant and Animal Structures“How do the parts of a plant or animal help it survive?”Solves a mystery from a soggy field notebook: how does a tall pink bird find food it can’t even see? Matches four Florida beaks to the tools they work like, sorts body parts as inside or outside the body, tests how deep three birds can wade, and builds a model alligator head to see why its eyes and nostrils sit on top. Then argues, with evidence, that plants have important structures too.Words: structure, function, internal, external, organism
Lesson 11.02Model: Structure and Survival“How can a model show why a structure helps an organism survive, not just what it’s called?”Helps a wildlife refuge pick one of three spoonbill drawings for a boardwalk sign: which one explains, not just names? Draws an arrow from an alligator’s teeth to its food, upgrades a labeled photo into a model one step at a time, builds part → action → need chains for four Florida organisms, then builds a model of a frog, tortoise, anhinga or manatee on a real photo.Words: model, adaptation, annotate, camouflage
Ask at dinner
“Which of your body parts works like a tool? What tool?” Then, after 11.02: “What’s the difference between a labeled drawing and a model?”
Try at home
Look at a pet or backyard bird. Pick one body part and guess its job together. In the kitchen, find a tool shaped like a beak: a spoon, tongs, a nutcracker.
12Week
Animals notice, decide and act in four steps.Senses to brain Field Station · new
Lesson 12.01Sensing the World“How do animals notice what’s happening around them, and decide what to do about it?”Solves a trail-camera mystery: four Florida animals reacted at almost the same time. Learns the four steps: stimulus → sense organ → brain → response.Words: stimulus, sense organ, brain, response, signal
Lesson 12.02Model: From Senses to Brain“How can a model show the way an animal turns information into action?”Solves the mystery of the silent camera: moths vanished in the dark, but a bat detector heard clicks. Finds moths with echoes, builds a strong model of a Florida bonneted bat’s hunt, matches four Florida animals to the senses they use, and fixes broken models. Then builds two models of its own and compares them.Words: pathway, process, model, scenario
Ask at dinner
“What would happen if an animal’s ears worked but its brain didn’t get the message?” Then, after 12.02: “How does a bat find a moth in the dark?” (Listen for: clicks, echoes, ears, brain.)
Try at home
Drop a spoon behind your student. Then name the four steps of what just happened: the sound, the ears, the brain, the jump. Or clap once in a big empty space, like a gym or a stairwell, and listen for the echo, the way a bat does.
13Week
Some traits come from parents. Others come from where you live.Passed down Field Station · new
Lesson 13.01Like Parent, Like Child“Which traits do Florida plants and animals get from their parents, and which come from where they live?”Solves the mystery of two gardens: seeds from one beach sunflower grew into a bushy plant in the sun and a thin one in the shade. Matches Florida babies to their parents, grows two seeds in different gardens, stamps four Florida traits as inherited, environmental or both, and predicts which of a panther mother’s traits her kittens will get. Then describes a real parent and its offspring.Words: trait, inherited, environment, offspring, resemble, variation
Lesson 13.02Flowering Plant Reproduction“How do Florida’s flowering plants make seeds, and how do those seeds become new plants?”Solves the mystery of the empty seed heads: a sunny garden bloomed but made almost no seeds. Flies a bee from flower to flower (watch out for look-alikes), runs a mesh-bag test to see what happens when bees can’t reach a flower, puts the five steps from pollen to new plant in order, and sorts five Florida seeds by how they travel. Then follows one Florida flower through all five steps.Words: flower, pollination, pollen, seed, fertilization, germination
Ask at dinner
“Is a dog’s fur color inherited or from its environment? What about how big it grows?” (Color is inherited. Size is both.) After 13.01: “Why did the two sunflowers look so different?” (Listen for: same parent, different sun.) After 13.02: “Why didn’t the garden make seeds?” (Listen for: no bees, no pollen moved, no seeds.)
Try at home
Look at a pet, a family photo or two plants of the same kind. Name one trait that came from the parents and one that came from life (a scar, a haircut, a sunburn). After 13.02, find a flower outside, look for the yellow pollen, and watch for a bee visiting it.
14Week
Living things grow in patterns and change with the seasons.Life cycles and seasons Field Station · new
Lesson 14.01Circle of Life“How are the life cycles of Florida plants and animals alike, and how are they different?”Solves a pond mystery: empty dragonfly skins on the cattails and a monarch chrysalis. Puts a monarch’s stages in order, watches a monarch and a grasshopper grow up side by side, closes the life-cycle circle for four Florida living things, and compares two of them, then writes one Florida life cycle.Words: life cycle, metamorphosis, stage, complete, incomplete, offspring
Lesson 14.02Model: Interdependence Across Seasons“How do Florida plants and animals depend on each other, and on the seasons, to survive?”Solves the mystery of the postcards from Maine: why is a Florida pond busy in January when a Maine pond is frozen and quiet? Sorts January photos into Florida or Maine, turns a calendar to see who stays, migrates or hibernates in each place, tests where manatees go when river water turns cold, and builds a two-region model with food-energy and migration arrows. Then builds a model of Florida and one other region.Words: seasonal change, interdependence, region, food energy, migration, hibernation
Ask at dinner
After 14.01: “Were the shells on the cattails dead bugs?” (Listen for: empty dragonfly skins.) After 14.02: “Does Florida have seasons?” (Listen for: yes, milder ones; ducks come here for food and open water.)
Try at home
Look for a caterpillar, a chrysalis or a butterfly on a milkweed or garden plant. In winter, count the ducks or robins at a nearby lake or park, and check again in summer.
15Week
People use things from Earth. Some run out.Earth’s resources Field Station · new
Lesson 15.01Renewable and Nonrenewable Resources“How do people use Earth’s resources, and which ones will run out?”Solves a mystery from space: satellite photos show square lakes and bare patches spreading across central Florida. Takes apart a pencil, a juice box and a Florida orange to see where each part came from, races time to see which resources come back (sunlight, water, pine forests) and which don’t (phosphate rock, oil), checks what using four real Florida resources changes, and compares two sources about phosphate mining. Then compares one renewable and one nonrenewable Florida resource.Words: resource, renewable, nonrenewable, impact, fossil fuel, source
Lesson 15.02Data Task: Local Resources“Why are some Florida resources found in one place and not another?”Tests a volunteer’s plan to dig for phosphate at four pins on a real Florida map. Guesses where four resources are before seeing the real map, turns map layers on one at a time to find each pattern (clustered, a band, along the coast), matches each pattern to its reason (old sea-floor rock, warm winters, the ocean, limestone close to the surface), visits a real Florida spring, and tests every pin. Then maps one Florida resource and explains its pattern.Words: availability, location, distribution, evidence, pattern, map key
Ask at dinner
After 15.01: “What made the square lakes?” (Listen for: phosphate mines; the pits fill with water, and the rock won’t come back.) “Is something renewable always good for the environment?” (Listen for: no, a solar farm still covers land.) After 15.02: “Why would phosphate mines be in one part of Florida and not all over?” (Listen for: phosphate rock formed only where ancient seas left it, and the map shows the mines clustered there.)
Try at home
Pick something in your kitchen and take it apart together: what is it made of, and did each part grow, come from the sky, or come out of the ground? Is each one renewable or not?

Ten Words That Carry the Unit

ProducerA living thing, usually a plant, that makes its own food using sunlight.
ConsumerA living thing that gets energy by eating other living things. Your student is one!
Food webAll the food chains in one place, connected together.
EcosystemAll the living and nonliving things in one place, like a wetland or a forest.
Structure & functionA body part (structure) and the job it does (function), like a pelican’s pouch scooping up fish.
Stimulus → responseSomething an animal notices, and what it does about it. The sense organ and brain are the steps in between.
Inherited traitA feature passed from parents to offspring, like a bird’s beak shape.
MetamorphosisA big change in an animal’s body as it grows, like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly.
ModelA drawing or build with labels and arrows that explains how something works.
RenewableA resource that comes back quickly enough to keep using, like sunlight or wind.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Vocabulary blanks, three short answers, a drawing or model, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 3 Assessment (about 60 minutes, “take it slow”): trace a Florida pine forest food web, explain a body part, build a deer’s four-step pathway, explain inherited and environmental traits, and write one CER about how people affect an ecosystem.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned with new Florida examples. Answers are typed on the page, gathered with one button, and pasted into Canvas.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 13 · Lesson 13.01
The warm-up mentions a family photo, and Part C asks for a real parent and its offspring. A pet, a backyard plant or an animal from the lesson works just as well; your student never has to write about their own family. If you’d like a different option, let the teacher know.
Week 13 · Going outside
In 13.01 your student can use a pet or a backyard plant for Part C, and in 13.02 an optional look at a real flower and its pollen. A photo or a picture online works fine if getting outside isn’t easy that day.
Week 10 · Lesson 10.02
Invasive species, like Burmese pythons and lionfish, harm Florida’s native animals. The lesson keeps it factual and focuses on what people are doing to help.
Week 15 · A new topic
The last week switches from living things to Earth’s resources. It still counts toward the Unit 3 Assessment, which covers Lessons 9.01 to 15.02.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What in the lesson shows that?”
  • Have your student explain their drawing to you out loud before writing about it.
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Point out Florida examples you see: a heron, a lizard, pollen on the car, a migrating flock.

Try to avoid

  • Drawing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
