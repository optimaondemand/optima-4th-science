About This Unit

This unit starts with one big idea, that almost all the energy in living things comes from the Sun, and builds from there: food chains and food webs, how an ecosystem stays healthy, how body parts help living things survive, how animals sense the world, how traits are passed down, and how life cycles and seasons repeat.

Almost every example is from Florida: the Everglades, pine forests, springs and beaches. The last week turns to the resources people use, like water, sunlight, oil and phosphate, and where they’re found in Florida.

By the end, your student should be able to trace energy from the Sun to an animal, explain how a body part helps a living thing survive, name the four steps an animal uses to react, and back up a claim with evidence.

“Energy in a food chain always starts with the Sun. Plants capture it. Animals borrow it by eating.”The big idea that runs through Unit 3, from Lesson 9.01

How a Week Works

Every week has two lessons on one topic. The first usually explains an idea; the second often has your student build a model (a labeled drawing that explains how something works) or work with real data. Each lesson follows the same path:

📚 VocabularyA quick warm-up and the day’s six words
📘 The LessonReading, pictures and short “Stop & Think” questions
🔬 ExploreA step-by-step activity in the notebook
🧠 Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
✍️ AssignmentShow what you know, then turn it in
  • ❓A Big Question at the top that the lesson answers by the end
  • 🔤Part A: four vocabulary blanks from a word bank
  • ✏️Part B: three short answers in complete sentences
  • 🎨Part C: a drawing, chart or model in the notebook
  • ⚖️Use Your Evidence: agree or disagree with a claim, with two pieces of evidence
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas
⚖️

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “Plants don’t really need sunlight; they get their food from the soil,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

The most common slip is giving an opinion with no evidence. A helpful question at home: “What in the lesson shows that?”

New: The Field Station (Lessons 10.01, 10.02 and 12.01)

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition. A field scientist, Dr. Lena Reyes, checks in over a walkie-talkie, and each lesson has a mystery to solve. In 10.01, a giant live oak falls in a storm and the whole forest around it changes. Why? In 10.02, airplane counts of manatees jump by 1,275 in a single year. Did that many really show up? In 12.01, four trail cameras catch four Florida animals reacting at almost the same moment. Why?

Short videos
As they’re recorded, quick videos from your student’s teacher walk through each part of the page.
Four clues
Each game your student finishes unlocks a clue. With all four, they crack the case.
Answers on the page

Dr. Reyes is a made-up character, but the animals, photos and science are real. Ask your student: “Did you crack the case? What were the clues?”

The Unit, Week by Week

Each week shows both lessons, the words your student will use, and two easy ways to keep the science going at home.

9Week
Every animal runs on energy that started in the Sun.Energy from the Sun
Lesson 9.01Energy Pathways: From Sun to Life“How does the energy from the Sun end up inside a gopher tortoise, a hawk, or you?”Traces one Everglades food chain step by step: Sun → sawgrass → grasshopper → tree frog → hawk.Words: energy, producer, consumer, photosynthesis, food chain, decomposer
Lesson 9.02The Power of Producers and Consumers“What happens to the rest of the food chain if one living thing disappears?”Predicts what happens to a wetland if all the green tree frogs disappear, then sees why real food chains connect into food webs.Words: food web, predator
Ask at dinner
“If all the grasshoppers disappeared, who would go hungry? Who might have too much to eat?”
Try at home
Pick something from dinner and trace it back to the Sun together. (Chicken → corn the chicken ate → Sun.)
10Week
Living things depend on each other in more ways than eating.A web of connections Field Station · new
Lesson 10.01Web of Life“What other ways are living things in a Florida forest connected, besides eating each other?”Solves a mystery: a giant live oak falls, and the owl, the squirrels and even the bears nearby are affected. Ties the oak to its neighbors, “pulls threads” in a web to predict what changes, and sorts a saw palmetto’s five kinds of connections: food, pollination, shelter, nutrients and human care.Words: ecosystem, habitat, pollination, pollinator, nutrients, web of life
Lesson 10.02Ecosystem Balance“How can we tell if a Florida ecosystem is healthy, and what can we do when it’s not?”Solves a second mystery: manatee counts from airplanes jumped by 1,275 in one year. Flies a practice survey to see how weather changes a count, stamps four Florida stories (manatees, pythons, lionfish, wood storks) as recovering or out of balance, reads 30 years of real survey flights, and builds a manatee line graph.Words: native species, invasive species, population, balance, conservation, human impact
Ask at dinner
“If one big tree fell in a forest, who would miss it, and why?” Then, after 10.02: “How do scientists know a change is real and not just a guess?” (Listen for: they collect numbers for a long time.)
Try at home
Look at one tree near home. Count the living things that use it: birds, bugs, lizards, moss. For 10.02: count the birds you see from one window for 5 minutes, two days in a row. Did the birds change, or did the weather?
11Week
Every body part has a job.Built to survive
Lesson 11.01Inside Out: Plant and Animal Structures“How do the parts of a plant or animal help it survive?”Studies an alligator’s snout, teeth, eyes and nostrils, then writes a Claim, Evidence and Reasoning about one of them.Words: structure, function, internal, external, organism
Lesson 11.02Model: Structure and Survival“How can a model show why a structure helps an organism survive, not just what it’s called?”Draws a Florida organism and adds arrows and short notes to show how one part does its job. A model explains; a label just names.Words: model, adaptation, annotate, camouflage
Ask at dinner
“What’s the difference between a labeled drawing and a model?”
Try at home
Look at a pet or backyard bird. Pick one body part and guess its job together.
12Week
Animals notice, decide and act in four steps.Senses to brain Field Station · new
Lesson 12.01Sensing the World“How do animals notice what’s happening around them, and decide what to do about it?”Solves a trail-camera mystery: four Florida animals reacted at almost the same time. Learns the four steps: stimulus → sense organ → brain → response.Words: stimulus, sense organ, brain, response, signal
Lesson 12.02Model: From Senses to Brain“How can a model show the way an animal turns information into action?”Builds two four-step models for two different animals (for example an owl that hears and a bear that smells) and compares them.Words: pathway, process, scenario
Ask at dinner
“What would happen if an animal’s ears worked but its brain didn’t get the message?”
Try at home
Drop a spoon behind your student. Then name the four steps of what just happened: the sound, the ears, the brain, the jump.
13Week
Some traits come from parents. Others come from where you live.Passed down
Lesson 13.01Like Parent, Like ChildFocus: Which traits come from parents, and which come from where a living thing lives?Sorts the traits of a pet, a plant or a backyard bird into inherited, environmental or both.Words: trait, inherited, environment, offspring, variation
Lesson 13.02Flowering Plant ReproductionFocus: How a flower makes seeds, and how seeds become new plants.Puts five steps in order (pollination, fertilization, seed, dispersal, germination), then looks at a real flower outside or in a photo.Words: pollen, pollination, fertilization, seed, germination
Ask at dinner
“Is a dog’s fur color inherited or from its environment? What about how big it grows?” (Color is inherited. Size is both.)
Try at home
Find a flower outside. Look for the yellow pollen, and watch for a bee or butterfly visiting it.
14Week
Living things grow in patterns and change with the seasons.Life cycles and seasons
Lesson 14.01Circle of Life“How are the life cycles of Florida plants and animals alike, and how are they different?”Compares two life cycles in a chart, one with metamorphosis (like a monarch) and one without (like a gopher tortoise).Words: life cycle, metamorphosis, stage, complete, incomplete
Lesson 14.02Model: Interdependence Across Seasons“How do Florida plants and animals depend on each other, and on the seasons, to survive?”Compares winter in Florida with winter somewhere colder, and shows why ducks and manatees come here for the winter.Words: seasonal change, interdependence, region, migration, hibernation
Ask at dinner
“Why do so many birds spend the winter in Florida?” (Listen for: food, warmth.)
Try at home
Look for a caterpillar, a chrysalis or a butterfly on a milkweed or garden plant.
15Week
People use things from Earth. Some run out.Earth’s resources
Lesson 15.01Renewable and Nonrenewable Resources“How do people use Earth’s resources, and which ones will run out?”Compares one resource that comes back (sun, wind, fresh water) with one that takes millions of years to form (oil, phosphate, limestone), using two sources.Words: resource, renewable, nonrenewable, fossil fuel, impact
Lesson 15.02Data Task: Local Resources“Why are some Florida resources found in one place and not another?”Reads a Florida resource map and uses a pattern on it (where citrus, springs or phosphate are) as evidence.Words: map key, pattern, distribution, location, evidence
Ask at dinner
“Why would phosphate mines be in one part of Florida and not all over?”
Try at home
Pick something in your kitchen and guess what it’s made from. Is that material renewable or not?

Ten Words That Carry the Unit

ProducerA living thing, usually a plant, that makes its own food using sunlight.
ConsumerA living thing that gets energy by eating other living things. Your student is one!
Food webAll the food chains in one place, connected together.
EcosystemAll the living and nonliving things in one place, like a wetland or a forest.
Structure & functionA body part (structure) and the job it does (function), like a pelican’s pouch scooping up fish.
Stimulus → responseSomething an animal notices, and what it does about it. The sense organ and brain are the steps in between.
Inherited traitA feature passed from parents to offspring, like a bird’s beak shape.
MetamorphosisA big change in an animal’s body as it grows, like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly.
ModelA drawing or build with labels and arrows that explains how something works.
RenewableA resource that comes back quickly enough to keep using, like sunlight or wind.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Vocabulary blanks, three short answers, a drawing or model, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 3 Assessment (about 60 minutes, “take it slow”): trace a Florida pine forest food web, explain a body part, build a deer’s four-step pathway, explain inherited and environmental traits, and write one CER about how people affect an ecosystem.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned with new Florida examples. Answers are typed on the page, gathered with one button, and pasted into Canvas.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 13 · Lesson 13.01
The warm-up uses a family photo, and the reflection asks your student to name a trait they got from a parent or grandparent. Any family member, a pet, or a plant they know well works just as well for this science. If you’d like a different option, let the teacher know.
Week 13 · Going outside
Two Explore activities ask your student to look closely at a real pet, backyard tree or flower. A photo or a picture online works fine if getting outside isn’t easy that day.
Week 10 · Lesson 10.02
Invasive species, like Burmese pythons and lionfish, harm Florida’s native animals. The lesson keeps it factual and focuses on what people are doing to help.
Week 15 · A new topic
The last week switches from living things to Earth’s resources. It still counts toward the Unit 3 Assessment, which covers Lessons 9.01 to 15.02.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What in the lesson shows that?”
  • Have your student explain their drawing to you out loud before writing about it.
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Point out Florida examples you see: a heron, a lizard, pollen on the car, a migrating flock.

Try to avoid

  • Drawing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
