About This Unit

This unit starts with motion. In week 25, your student learns to describe how something moved by comparing it to a reference point (something that stays put), and then to measure speed using distance and time.

Week 26 is about waves: how a wave carries energy along while the water or rope mostly stays in place, and how patterns of light or sound can carry a coded message. Weeks 27 and 28 turn to matter, the stuff everything is made of. Your student describes materials by their properties, sorts them, and tests which ones a magnet pulls on.

By the end, your student should be able to describe motion clearly enough for someone else to draw it, compare speeds with data, explain what a wave carries, and use properties and tests to classify materials.

“Compared to what?”The question that runs through Unit 6

How a Week Works

Each week has two lessons on one topic. Often the first explains an idea and the second is an investigation or engineering challenge, where your student measures, tests or designs something. Each lesson follows the same path:

🌅 Warm-UpA quick start and the day’s six words
📘 The LessonReading, pictures and short games or activities
🧠 Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
✍️ AssignmentShow what you know, then turn it in
  • ❓A Big Question at the top that the lesson answers by the end
  • ✏️Part B: short answers in complete sentences
  • 🔬Part C: a bigger task, like drawing a motion diagram of something at home
  • 🗣️Use Your Evidence: agree or disagree with a claim, with two pieces of evidence
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas

Lessons are being updated to the Field Station style one at a time. Until a lesson is updated, it has a fifth tab called Explore between the Lesson and the Self-Check, and it may ask for a notebook. Typed answers still gather at the bottom of the Assignment tab.

⚖️

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “You can describe motion without a reference point. Just say how far it moved,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

In this unit, the best evidence often comes from a measurement or a test. A helpful question at home: “What did the test show?”

The Field Station: Sam Park Is Back

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition with a mystery to solve. Week 25 stays with Sam Park, the engineer (someone who designs and builds things to solve problems) from Unit 5, at Palmetto Beach. In 25.01, it is the morning after the boat parade. Teo has buried a time capsule in the sand and drawn a treasure map, but his directions start at a rowboat and never say which way to walk. Six people have dug six holes. What is wrong with the directions, and what would fix them? In 25.02, the hot-dog stand’s robot, Zippy, has a sign that says “FASTEST ON THE BEACH! 90 steps!” Should Rosa trade Sam’s robot, Scoot, for Zippy? Your student finds out what it really takes to prove who is faster. More cases arrive as each lesson of the unit is finished.

The Unit, Week by Week

Each week shows both lessons, the words your student will use, and two easy ways to keep the science going at home.

25Week
To describe how something moved, compare it to something that stays put.Position, motion and speed Field Station · 25.01 and 25.02
Lesson 25.01How Do Objects Move?“How can we describe an object’s motion — where it started, where it ended, and which way it went?”Solves Case 25, “The Lost Time Capsule”: Teo buried a time capsule on Palmetto Beach and wrote “Start at the rowboat. Walk 5 steps. Dig!” Six people dug and found nothing. Looks out a train window and votes on which train moved (the station sign settles it), finds lost things on a beach map from position clues (the rowboat clue leads to an empty square, because the rowboat moved), lines up direction cards for Scoot the delivery robot (which does exactly what the words say), and builds motion diagrams of a crab and a seagull. A surprise in the middle: a real photo of a Ferris wheel whose lights draw the path of every rider. After the case, rewrites Teo’s directions so they still work next year. Tool of the Week: a compass.Words: position, direction, motion, reference point, at rest, path
Lesson 25.02Investigation: Measuring Speed“How can we use distance and time to tell which object moved FASTER?”Solves Case 25, part 2, “Zippy vs Scoot”: Zippy went 90 steps and Scoot went 60, but does farther mean faster? Fixes Zippy’s sign with word magnets so it only says what the number proves, calls races on scratch-off tickets before and after the hidden numbers appear, cuts each robot’s run into 10-second pieces to compare them fairly, and runs a fair rematch with one start and one finish line, typing the race-clock times into a data table. A surprise in the middle: a model photo-finish camera, then a real photo finish, where the picture shows time instead of distance. After the case, fills in Rosa’s robot report and spots the head start in a race Zippy “won”.Words: speed, distance, time, device, fair test, data
Ask at dinner
After 25.01: “Why couldn’t anyone find the time capsule?” (Listen for: the rowboat moved, so it was a bad reference point, and the directions never said which way to walk.) “If you get off a Ferris wheel right where you got on, did you move?” (Listen for: yes, your position kept changing; your path was a circle.) After 25.02: “What two things do you need to know to tell how fast something went?” (Listen for: how far it went and how long it took.) “Zippy went farther than Scoot. Does that make Zippy faster?” (Listen for: not by itself; Zippy also took much longer.)
Try at home
Hide a small “treasure” and write directions to it. Start from something that stays put, like the front door, and give a direction and a number of steps. Can someone in the family find it? For 25.02: race two toy cars or balls down a book ramp from the same start to the same finish line, time each one with a phone stopwatch, and make a four-column table: object, distance, time, notes.
26Week
Waves carry energy, and patterns can carry a message.Waves and codes
Lesson 26.01Waves and How They Travel“How does a wave carry energy from one place to another without the water (or slinky) itself going on a journey?”Models a wave with a slinky or a rope and labels its parts. Explains that a wave moves energy along, while the water or rope mostly stays where it is.Words: wave, amplitude, wavelength, medium, crest
Lesson 26.02Engineering: Coded Messages“How can we use PATTERNS to send a message someone can decode?”Designs a code made of patterns, such as long and short flashes or sounds. Tests it with a partner, finds where the message went wrong, and redesigns it.Words: pattern, code, transmit, constraint, redesign
Ask at dinner
After 26.01: “When a wave passes a floating duck, does the duck travel along with the wave?” (Listen for: no, it bobs up and down; the wave’s energy moves on.) After 26.02: “How could you send a message across a dark yard with a flashlight?” (Listen for: a code, like long and short flashes that stand for letters.)
Try at home
Tie a jump rope to a doorknob and shake the other end. Try small shakes, then big ones. What changes? Then invent a flashlight or tapping code for three words and see if a family member can decode it.
27Week
Everything is made of matter, and its properties tell us what it is good for.Matter and properties
Lesson 27.01What Is Matter?“How can we describe and measure the “stuff” that everything is made of?”Describes objects by properties that can be observed (color, texture, hardness) and properties that can be measured (mass, volume).Words: matter, mass, volume, observable, measurable
Lesson 27.02Classify by Properties“How can we use properties to sort materials and pick the right one for a job?”Sorts materials by their properties and chooses the best material for a job, with a reason.Words: classify, property, material, flexible, waterproof
Ask at dinner
After 27.01: “Tell me three properties of your cereal bowl.” (Listen for: things you can observe or measure, like hard, smooth, round, how heavy it is.) After 27.02: “Why is a raincoat made of plastic and not paper?” (Listen for: plastic is waterproof and flexible.)
Try at home
Go on a property hunt: find something flexible, something waterproof, something hard and something see-through. Then pick one job (a lunch bag, a pet bed) and choose the best material for it. Why that one?
28Week
Magnets pull on some materials and not others, and a test can prove which.Magnets
Lesson 28.01Magnets Attract and Repel“How do magnets pull on some things and push on others — and how can we predict which?”Explores what magnets attract, and how two magnets can pull together or push apart.Words: magnet, attract, repel, magnetic, magnetic field
Lesson 28.02Investigation: Testing Magnets“What EVIDENCE shows whether a material is magnetic — and how do we test it like a scientist?”Predicts, then tests eight materials with a magnet, records the results and uses them as evidence.Words: magnetic, nonmagnetic, prediction, attract, repel
Ask at dinner
After 28.01: “Do magnets stick to every metal?” (Listen for: no. Iron and steel, yes. Aluminum foil and most coins, no.) After 28.02: “Why predict before you test?” (Listen for: so you can check your thinking against what really happens.)
Try at home
Take a fridge magnet around the kitchen. Pick eight things, predict “sticks” or “doesn’t stick” for each, then test. Which results surprised you?

Ten Words That Carry the Unit

Reference pointSomething that stays put, which you compare to.
PositionWhere something is, compared to a reference point.
SpeedHow fast something moves. You find it from distance and time.
WaveA repeating movement that carries energy from place to place.
AmplitudeHow tall a wave is, from its resting line to its top.
CodeA set of patterns that stand for letters or words.
MatterAnything that has mass and takes up space.
MassHow much matter is in an object.
PropertySomething you can observe or measure about a material, like its color, its hardness or whether it bends.
Attract / repelTo attract is to pull toward. To repel is to push away. Magnets can do both.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Short answers, a bigger Part C task, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 6 Assessment: Motion, Waves, Matter & Magnets (take it slow): use a reference point and distance and time data to describe motion and speed; explain waves, amplitude and a flashlight code; predict what magnets do; describe matter by its properties; and write a claim with evidence.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned in Lessons 25.01 to 28.02. Answers are typed and submitted in Canvas.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 25 · Something that moves
Part C of 25.01 asks your student to watch something move at home (a toy car, a ball or a pet) and draw its motion diagram on the page. For 25.02 Part C, two things that roll, a book for a ramp and a phone stopwatch help (or your student can use the numbers from the on-page rematch).
Week 28 · Magnets
A fridge magnet is all your student needs. Keep magnets away from phones, tablets and bank cards. Small, strong magnets are dangerous if swallowed, so please keep them away from young children.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What did the test show?”
  • When something moves, ask: “Compared to what?” and “Which way did it go?”
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Encourage a prediction before every test. A wrong prediction is still useful.

Try to avoid

  • Wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
