📘 The Lesson: When Two Things Bump
Motion and energy switch places
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Collisions Are Energy Handoffs

A collisionCollisionWhen two objects bump into each other. happens any time two objects bump into each other. A bowling ball hitting pins. Two pool balls clicking. Your foot kicking a soccer ball. Your bumper meeting a curb. They're all collisions.

And every collision does the same basic thing: it transfersTransferWhen energy moves between objects. energy and motion from one object to another. A moving ball stops or slows down; a still object starts moving. The total energy goes somewhere — it doesn't just disappear.

🛑
Stop & Think
"A moving marble hits a still marble. Right after the collision, where is the motion? Where did the moving marble's energy go?"

Part 2 · Three Kinds of Motion Change

When objects collide, both can experience motion changeMotion changeA shift in how an object moves. in different ways. Here are the three patterns you'll see most:

  • One moving, one still: The moving object slows down or stops. The still object starts moving. (Example: cue ball hits an 8-ball.)
  • Both moving in the same direction: The fast one slows; the slow one speeds up. (Example: a fast car bumping a slower car ahead of it.)
  • Both moving toward each other: Both slow down — and sometimes change direction. (Example: two soccer players running into each other.)

If you know what BEFORE looks like — which objects were moving, and how fast — you can usually make a good predictionPredictionWhat you think will happen. about AFTER. Comparing before/afterBefore/AfterCompare what changed. is the easiest way to spot a collision's effects.

Common misconception: Some people think the moving object always "wins" a collision and the still object always "loses." In reality, BOTH change motion in most collisions — and energy moves between them, not just from one to the other.

Amazing Florida Fact
Modern Florida car bumpers are designed to crumple on purpose. When a car crashes, the bumper absorbs the collision's energy — that's why your bumper might look ruined after a fender-bender, but you're fine. The bumper did its job by absorbing the energy.
🌴

Florida connection: Every Florida bowling alley is a mini physics lab. The heavier the ball, the more energy it carries. The faster the ball, the more energy it carries. Strikes happen when ALL that energy transfers into the pins at the perfect angle — and the pins crash into each other in a chain reaction.

🔭 Go Deeper · What's an "elastic" collision?
Scientists describe collisions as more or less elastic. An ELASTIC collision (like a pool ball hitting another pool ball) transfers most of the energy as motion — almost none gets lost to heat or sound. An INELASTIC collision (like a car crash) loses a lot of energy to deformation, heat, and sound. Real-life collisions usually fall somewhere in between.

🔬 Explore: Predict the Collision
Use what you know to guess before you test
⏱ 8 min

Grab two same-sized balls (marbles, ping-pong balls, anything). By step 4 you'll have a before/after sketch and a prediction tested against real evidence.

1
Step 1
Set up your collision

Place one ball still on a table. Roll the other ball straight at it from a few inches away. Watch what happens.

2
Step 2
Sketch BEFORE

Before you collide them again, sketch the BEFORE picture in your notebook. Label which ball is moving, and which is still.

3
Step 3
Predict AFTER

Now PREDICT — sketch what you think the AFTER will look like. Will both balls move? Will one stop? Write your prediction in one sentence.

4
Step 4
Run the test & compare

Roll the moving ball into the still one. Sketch the REAL AFTER. Compare to your prediction — were you right? Where did the motion and energy go?

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
In the time it takes you to blink, a bowling ball can hit pins and finish a strike — a collision with so much energy that scientists can predict the exact pattern the pins will fall in.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"A Florida scientist studies what happens when two cars meet head-on in a crash test. What three kinds of evidence would they look at AFTER the collision to understand what really happened?"
🔭 Go Deeper · Why do collisions make sound?
When two objects collide, the impact makes them vibrate. Those vibrations push the air around them, which our ears hear as sound. So a collision's energy doesn't just go into motion — some becomes sound vibrations. The harder the collision, the louder the sound.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

What's a COLLISION?

AWhen an object stops on its own
BWhen two objects bump into each other
CWhen sunlight hits a leaf
💥 Right! A collision is two objects bumping. It's one way energy transfers from one object to another.
Objects don't stop on their own (something has to push them), and sunlight hitting a leaf is a light transfer, not a collision. A collision is two solid objects bumping into each other.
Q2

A moving marble hits a still marble. The moving marble stops; the still marble moves. What happened to the energy?

AIt transferred from the moving marble to the still one
BIt disappeared
CIt doubled
🎱 Right! Energy doesn't disappear — it transferred from one marble to the other.
Energy doesn't disappear or double in a collision. It moves — in this case from the moving marble into the still one (with maybe a little turning into sound and heat).
Q3

What's the BEST way to predict what will happen in a collision?

AGuess randomly
BWait until after
CLook at the BEFORE — what's moving, how fast, and what they'll hit
🎯 Right! Knowing the BEFORE lets you make a good prediction about the AFTER.
Predictions are based on what you can OBSERVE before the collision happens. If you know what's moving and how, you can usually predict the result.
