About This Unit

This short unit is about choices. In week 16, your student learns that using any resource is a tradeoff: people gain something (a benefit), the environment loses something (an environmental cost), and a protection strategy can make that cost smaller. Then they think like engineers: list what a good solution must do, compare a few ideas, pick one and improve it.

In week 17, the choices get bigger. Florida has hurricanes, floods, sinkholes and wildfires. Engineers can’t stop nature, but they can reduce the harm with smart design. Your student traces what a storm changes, then compares ways to protect a town.

By the end, your student should be able to name the benefit, cost and protection strategy of a resource choice, trace a natural event’s effects on land, living things and people, and compare two solutions and recommend one, with evidence.

“Every choice has two sides. The job is to weigh them, then find a way to make the cost smaller.”The big idea that runs through Unit 4, from the case in Lesson 16.01

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “Every Florida resource decision should choose what’s best for the environment, no matter what,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

In this unit there’s often no single right side. What matters is the evidence. A helpful question at home: “What in the lesson shows that?”

The Field Station: Meet June Calloway

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition with a mystery to solve. This unit’s scientist is June Calloway, a geologist (a scientist who studies rocks and the land) who works in the small town of Palmetto Springs. In 16.01, a company wants to dig a limestone quarry (a big pit where rock is dug out) next to the town’s wetland. Should the town say yes, and what would make it worth it? In the review day 17.R1, a hole opens on Main Street after a hurricane. Did the storm really do it? In 17.R2, seawater floods Bay Street, and the town has three plans and one budget. Which plan should they build?

The Unit, Week by Week

Each week shows both lessons, the words your student will use, and two easy ways to keep the science going at home.

Lesson 16.01Modern Resources & Environmental Choices“When we use a resource, how do we balance what we GAIN against what we LOSE?”Meets June Calloway and solves Case 16: should Palmetto Springs say yes to a rock quarry beside its wetland? Sorts what a shower, toast and a car ride gain and use up, loads the quarry’s benefits and costs onto a balance scale, sorts real Florida protection strategies into four kinds (conservation, restoration, better technology, rules), visits a real old quarry in the Florida Keys, and runs a town hall. Bonus: a water meter that counts how much water a shower, a drip and a lawn sprinkler use.Words: tradeoff, benefit, environmental cost, protection strategy, solution, conservation
Lesson 16.02Engineering: Improve a Resource Solution“How can we compare different solutions for using resources, and pick the best one to revise and improve?”A Florida city needs more clean water: drill new wells, build a plant that takes salt out of seawater, or set rules to use less? Lists what a good solution must do (criteria) and the limits it must fit (constraints), compares ideas side by side in a decision matrix, then improves the winner.Words: criteria, constraint, design, solution, revise, evaluate
Ask at dinner
After 16.01: “Should Palmetto Springs say yes to the quarry?” (Listen for: yes, but only with protection strategies; the cost gets smaller, but never zero.) After 16.02: “Why do engineers compare more than one idea?” (Listen for: the first idea isn’t always the best fit, and the winner can still be improved.)
Try at home
Pick a family choice, like driving or walking to the park, and list both sides together: what you gain and what you use up. Or look at your water bill together: how much water did your home use last month? Pick one way to use less.

Ten Words That Carry the Unit

TradeoffWhen you gain one thing but give up another.
BenefitWhat people gain by using a resource, like energy, food, jobs or building stone.
Environmental costWhat the environment loses when people use a resource, like habitat, clean water or clean air.
Protection strategyA choice or rule that makes the environmental cost smaller.
CriteriaWhat a good solution needs to do: the goals.
ConstraintA limit a solution must fit, like a budget or a law.
Natural hazardA natural event that can harm people, land or animals, like a hurricane, flood, sinkhole or wildfire.
Cause & effectA cause makes something happen. The effect is the change it makes.
MitigationMaking the harm from a hazard smaller, like building homes higher where floods happen.
ReviseTo change and improve a design based on what you learned.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Short answers, a bigger Part C task, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 4 Assessment: Engineer a Safer Florida (about 45–60 minutes, “take it slow”): trace a natural hazard’s cause and effects on land, living things and people; compare two solutions using criteria and constraints; recommend one with evidence and suggest a way to make it stronger.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned in Lessons 16.01 to 17.02. Answers are typed on the page, gathered with one button, and pasted into Canvas.

💛

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 17 · Storms and hazards
Lessons 17.01 and 17.02 and both review days talk about hurricanes, floods and sinkholes. The focus is on how people stay safe and how engineers reduce harm. If your family has been through a big storm, your student may want to talk about it, and that’s a great time to listen.
Week 16 · No single right answer
Many questions this unit ask what a town should do. Either side can be a strong answer when it’s backed by evidence from the lesson.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What in the lesson shows that?”
  • When your family makes a choice, name both sides out loud: what you gain and what it costs.
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Point out engineering you see: a raised house, a seawall, a storm drain, a solar panel.

Try to avoid

  • Wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
