📘 The Lesson: When a Link is Missing
What really happens when something disappears from a food chain
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Every Link Depends on Another

In Lesson 9.01 you traced the path of energyEnergyThe ability to do work or cause change. from the Sun, through a producerProducerA living thing — usually a plant — that makes its own food using sunlight., and into each consumerConsumerA living thing that gets energy by eating other living things.. Today you'll look closer at what holds the chain together.

A food chain isn't just a list of animals. It's a chain of dependencies. The frog depends on the grasshopper. The grasshopper depends on the sawgrass. Take away any one link and the whole chain feels it.

🛑
Stop & Think
"In one Florida wetland, all the grasshoppers suddenly die from a disease. Who do you think is hit first — the sawgrass, the frogs, or the hawks? Make a quick prediction before reading on."

Part 2 · Real Food Chains Are Webs

Most animals don't eat just one thing. A red-shouldered hawk eats frogs, but it also eats lizards, mice, and small snakes. A green tree frog eats grasshoppers, but it also eats moths, spiders, and beetles. When you connect all these eating relationships together, you get a food webFood webAll the food chains in one place, connected together..

Because of food webs, removing one link doesn't always wipe out an ecosystem — animals can sometimes switch to a different food source. But it always causes change. Some animals lose meals. Some lose a predatorPredatorAn animal that hunts and eats other animals.. Some plants get eaten more.

Common misconception: Some people think "if a predator disappears, the prey will just keep growing forever." That's not quite right. Without predators, the prey usually grows quickly at first — then runs out of food and crashes. The system corrects itself, but not gently.

⭐ Key Concept

Every link in a food chain matters. Remove a producer, and consumers lose food. Remove a predator, and prey can grow out of balance.

Amazing Florida Fact
When wolves were brought back to Yellowstone, the elk stopped overgrazing, willow trees grew back along streams, and even the rivers changed shape. One missing predator was changing the whole landscape.
🌴

Florida connection: Burmese pythons aren't native to the Everglades, but they got loose and started eating everything — marsh rabbits, raccoons, even small alligators. Now scientists are watching the food web shift around them. Some Florida ecosystems have lost most of their small mammals because of this one missing-link problem in reverse.

🔭 Go Deeper · What is a "keystone species"?
Some animals have an extra-big effect on their food web. Scientists call these keystone species — like the keystone in a stone archway that holds the whole thing up. In Florida, the American alligator is a keystone species. It digs deep "gator holes" that hold water during droughts, helping fish, turtles, and birds survive. Without alligators, the wetland would be a very different place.

🔬 Explore: Investigating the Missing Link
Predict, observe, and explain — step by step
⏱ 8 min

We're going to investigate one scenario step by step: What happens to the wetland if all the green tree frogs disappear? Sketch each step in your notebook. By the end, you'll have evidence to support a prediction.

1
Step 1
Sketch the starting chain

Draw a quick food chain in your notebook: sawgrass → grasshopper → green tree frog → red-shouldered hawk. Put arrows between each one. This is the "before" picture — everything in the chain is still alive.

2
Step 2
Predict before you remove

Before changing anything, write down two predictions: (1) What do you think will happen to the hawk? (2) What do you think will happen to the grasshopper? Be specific — say "more," "fewer," or "the same."

3
Step 3
Cross out the frog

In your sketch, draw a big X through the green tree frog. The frog is gone. Now follow the arrows. The arrow into the frog (from grasshoppers) — what happens to it? The arrow out of the frog (to the hawk) — what happens to it?

4
Step 4
Compare with evidence

Now look at the diagram above and tap the green tree frog. Read what really happens. Compare it to your prediction. Were you right? Partly right? Different? Write one sentence about what surprised you most.

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
When the gopher tortoise digs its burrow, more than 350 other species use that burrow for shelter — owls, snakes, mice, even other tortoises. If gopher tortoises disappear, hundreds of species lose a home all at once.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"Scientists test predictions with evidence. Look at your sketch and the diagram together. What evidence did you use to make your prediction? What evidence would change your mind?"
🔭 Go Deeper · Why can ecosystems sometimes "bounce back"?
A food web has lots of paths, not just one chain. So if one link is removed, animals can sometimes switch to other foods — a hawk that eats frogs can also eat mice and snakes. That's why some ecosystems bounce back faster than others. The wider the food web, the more "backup" there is. But every change in the web still has a cost.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

In a wetland, all the sawgrass dies from a flood. Which animal feels the change first?

AThe red-shouldered hawk
BThe grasshopper
CThe decomposer
🦗 Right! The grasshopper eats the sawgrass directly, so it's the first one to lose food.
Think about who eats the sawgrass directly. The grasshopper is the first link affected — the hawk feels it later as the chain shifts.
Q2

A food web is different from a food chain because…

AIt only shows plants
BIt has no arrows
CIt connects many food chains together
🕸️ Right! A food web shows that most animals eat — and are eaten by — more than one kind of thing.
Not quite. A food web is a bigger picture: it shows lots of food chains laid on top of each other, with arrows crossing between them.
Q3

If the hawk (top predator) disappears from the wetland, what is most likely to happen first?

AThere will be more tree frogs because nothing is hunting them
BThe sawgrass will die right away
CNothing will change
🦅 Right! Take away the predator, and prey numbers usually grow first — until they run out of food.
Think about who the hawk was eating. With no hawk hunting frogs, the frogs aren't being eaten — so their numbers tend to go up first. The other effects come later.
