🔑 Teacher Answer Key

Suggested answers, scoring notes, and how to apply the common rubric.

Scoring approach. Each question shows ONE possible strong answer. Student answers may use different wording but should hit the same key ideas. Use the score guide under each question to decide level: 0 = blank/incorrect, 1 = partial, 2 = full.

Part A · Observations & Inferences

Q1

Sort the 6 notebook entries as observation (O) or inference (I).

Key
1 = O · 2 = I · 3 = O · 4 = I · 5 = O · 6 = I
All ODD rows are direct observations (something they noticed). All EVEN rows add a guess or interpretation based on what they noticed.
Scoring1 point per correct sort (6 max). 5–6 correct = Proficient; 3–4 = Developing; 0–2 = Beginning.
Q2

Pick one inference and explain WHY.

Sample answer
"I marked row 4 as an inference because the student saw stars in a line (the observation) and then GUESSED it was the Big Dipper based on the shape they recognized. The guess uses the observation plus knowledge they already had."
ScoringProficient: clearly explains the observation vs. the added guess. Developing: identifies a guess without naming what made it a guess.
Q3

Match each situation to the right tool and explain why.

Key
1. Mars surface — Telescope. Mars is too far to visit; a telescope collects light from far-away things.
2. Wildflower petals — Hand lens. The petals are small; a hand lens magnifies tiny details.
3. Sun direction — Compass. A compass tells you which way is north so you can name the direction the Sun rose from.
ScoringTool match worth 1pt each (3 max). Reasoning worth 1pt each (3 max). Full = 6/6.

Part B · Sky & Moon Patterns

Q4

Describe the pattern in the moon data.

Sample answer
"Each night the lit-up part of the moon gets a little bigger, from a thin sliver on night 1 to a full circle on night 6. Then on night 7 it starts to get smaller again, but the unlit part now shows on the right side."
ScoringMust describe an INCREASE in lit area across the week. Bonus credit for noticing the change on night 7.
Q5

Waxing or waning during the first 6 nights, and how do you know?

Sample answer
"The moon is WAXING. I can tell because the lit part gets BIGGER each night — sliver → bigger sliver → half → more than half → almost full → full."
ScoringProficient: names "waxing" + cites the table evidence. Developing: names "waxing" without table evidence.
Q6

Is the moon making its own light?

Sample answer
"My friend is wrong. The moon doesn't make its own light. The Sun shines on the moon, and we see the lit-up part as the moon's phases. That's why we sometimes see only a sliver — only part of the moon's lit side is facing us."
ScoringProficient: clearly states the moon doesn't make light + names the Sun as the light source. Developing: only says "no" without explaining.
Q7

Which star helps a Florida sailor find north at night?

Sample answer
"The sailor should look for POLARIS, the North Star, because it stays in the same spot in the sky each night and always points to north. Sailors have used it for thousands of years."
ScoringMust name Polaris/North Star AND explain why it works (stays put or always points north). 2 pts.
Q8

Which Earth motion causes day/night? Which causes the seasons?

Key
Day and night is caused by Earth's rotation (it spins once every 24 hours). The seasons are caused by Earth's revolution around the Sun, combined with the tilt of Earth's axis.
Scoring1 pt for each correct match. Accept "spinning" for rotation, "going around the Sun" for revolution.

Part C · CER + Reflection

CER · Claim

How do scientists learn about things they can't visit directly?

Sample claim
"Scientists learn about far-away things by using tools and models that bring the information to them."
CER · Evidence
Sample evidence
Evidence 1: "Telescopes collect light from very far away — that's how we know what the moon's surface looks like up close."
Evidence 2: "Scientists keep records of the moon over time and notice it follows the same lunar cycle. The pattern is evidence."
CER · Reasoning
Sample reasoning
"My evidence supports my claim because both examples — using a telescope to see the moon, and noticing the lunar pattern over time — show that scientists use TOOLS and PATTERNS to learn about things they can't visit. They don't have to go to the moon to know how it works."
CER Scoring4 pts: Claim 1pt · Evidence 2pts (1 per piece) · Reasoning 1pt. Advanced students may bring in additional ideas (light-years, modeling, etc.).
Q9 · Reflection

What did you think a scientist was before, and now?

Sample answer
"I used to think a scientist was someone in a white coat working in a lab. Now I think a scientist is anyone who observes carefully, uses evidence, and stays curious. You can be a scientist outside at night looking at the moon."
ScoringOpen-ended. Look for honest reflection that names some shift in understanding. Full credit for any thoughtful contrast.

Common misconceptions to watch for:
  • Confusing "the moon makes light" — clarify it's reflected sunlight.
  • Confusing rotation with revolution — give a quick demo with a flashlight.
  • Treating an inference like a fact — emphasize that inferences are educated guesses, not certainties.
