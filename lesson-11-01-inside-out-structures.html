📘 The Lesson: Body Parts Are Tools
Every structure has a job — let's match them up
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Structure Meets Function

Every part of a plant or animal is a kind of tool. A structureStructureA body part of a plant or animal. is the part itself — a leaf, a beak, a paw, a root. The functionFunctionThe job a structure does. is the job that part does. Structure is the thing. Function is what the thing does.

A leaf is a structure. Its function is to catch sunlight and make food for the plant. A hawk's sharp talons are a structure. Their function is to grab prey. Every organismOrganismAny single living thing. is built from dozens of structures, each doing a job that helps it survive.

🛑
Stop & Think
"Pick one body part you used today — your teeth, your hands, your eyes. What is its function? Now imagine if that part were a different shape. Would it still do the same job?"

Part 2 · Inside and Outside

Structures come in two kinds. ExternalExternalOutside the body. structures are the parts you can see from the outside — skin, fur, feathers, leaves, shells, bark, claws. InternalInternalInside the body. structures are inside — hearts, lungs, bones, the inside of a stem.

  • External plant structures: leaves, bark, flowers, thorns, fruit.
  • Internal plant structures: the inside of the stem that carries water; the inside of a seed.
  • External animal structures: beaks, claws, feathers, shells, eyes, ears.
  • Internal animal structures: heart, lungs, stomach, bones.

Both kinds matter. A turtle's shell (external) protects it from predators. The turtle's lungs (internal) let it breathe air, even underwater for short trips. Take away either one, and survivalSurvivalStaying alive. gets a lot harder.

Common misconception: Some people think a structure exists "in order to" help an animal — like the animal asked for it. That's not quite right. Structures developed slowly, over many generations, because the animals with the most useful structures were the ones most likely to survive and have babies. The structure didn't plan ahead — it just turned out to work.

🌵 Plant Survival Adaptations

Plant structures aren't random — they're adaptations that help plants survive in their specific environment. A cactus in a desert has thick, waxy skin to hold water and sharp spines instead of leaves to keep animals from eating it. A mangrove tree along Florida's coast has tangled roots that rise above the water, helping it breathe in salty, muddy soil. A Venus flytrap in poor soil traps insects to get the nutrients the soil can't provide.

Each of these structures solves a survival problem in that plant's habitat. When you look at a plant, ask: "What challenge does this plant face — and which structure helps it overcome that challenge?"

Amazing Florida Fact
A bald eagle's eye is so sharp it can spot a rabbit from over a mile away — about 4 to 8 times stronger than your eyes. Same structure (an eye), totally different function.
🌴

Florida connection: The Florida gopher tortoise digs burrows up to 40 feet long using its strong, shovel-shaped front legs. More than 350 other Florida species — owls, snakes, mice, lizards — use those burrows for shelter. One tortoise's legs end up being a structure that helps a whole ecosystem.

🔭 Go Deeper · How do scientists find hidden internal structures?
You can't cut open a living animal to find out what's inside. So scientists use X-rays, CT scans, and ultrasound to see internal structures without harming the animal. That's how veterinarians can check a manatee's lungs or look at a sea turtle's shell from the inside.

🔬 Explore: Match Structures to Jobs
Use evidence to argue why a structure helps an organism survive
⏱ 8 min

We'll investigate one Florida animal — the American alligator — and make an evidence-based argument about one of its structures. By step 4, you'll have your own CER (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning).

1
Step 1
Observe the structure

In your notebook, sketch an alligator's mouth. Pay attention to: the shape of the snout (long and wide), the size of the teeth, the position of the eyes and nostrils (high on the head). These are external structures.

2
Step 2
Make a claim

Pick ONE structure from your sketch. Write a claim: "The alligator's _______ helps it survive by _______." Use a vocab word from today.

3
Step 3
Add your evidence

Now add a sentence starting with "The evidence is…" Describe what you see — the shape, position, or size of the structure. Don't guess; describe.

4
Step 4
Reason it out

Finish with one more sentence: "This means…" — explain how that structure helps the alligator survive in a Florida swamp. (Hint: think about hunting, breathing, hiding, or protection.)

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
Saw palmetto leaves can live for more than 7 years. And the saw palmetto plant itself can live for 500 to 700 years — using the same root structure that whole time.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"A scientist finds an unfamiliar Florida bird with webbed feet, a long pointy beak, and oily feathers. Without watching it eat or move, what could you predict about how and where it lives? What evidence in the structures helped you decide?"
🔭 Go Deeper · Why do similar animals have different beaks?
Florida is home to many kinds of birds, and you can usually guess what they eat just by looking at their beaks. Cardinals have short, thick beaks for cracking seeds. Hummingbirds have long, thin beaks for sipping nectar. Anhingas have sharp, pointed beaks like spears for catching fish. Same body part — different shape — different function.

Q1

A leaf is a structure. What is its main function?

ATo hold water for the plant
BTo catch sunlight so the plant can make food
CTo anchor the plant in the soil
🌿 Right! Leaves catch sunlight, which the plant uses to make its own food.
That's the job of a different part. Roots hold the plant in the soil. Leaves are mostly about catching sunlight to make food.
Q2

A gopher tortoise's hard shell is an example of which kind of structure?

AExternal
BInternal
CInvasive
🐢 Right! You can see the shell from the outside — that makes it external.
Look at where the shell is — you can see it from the outside of the tortoise. That makes it an external structure.
Q3

A roseate spoonbill's beak is wide and flat. What's the best evidence-based argument for why?

ABecause the bird thought it would look pretty
BThe flat shape helps it sweep through shallow water and feel small prey
CIt is an invasive species in Florida
🦩 Right! The shape matches the job. The flat beak sweeps water and feels prey the bird can't see.
A good evidence-based argument connects the shape of the structure to the job it does. The spoonbill's flat beak is built for sweeping shallow water and feeling food.
