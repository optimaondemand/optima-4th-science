📘 The Lesson: Predict, Test, Compare
Three-step investigation cycle
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Why Predict BEFORE You Test?

A predictionPredictionWhat you think will happen. is your best guess about what will happen, written down BEFORE you run a test. That timing matters: a prediction you write first can be checked. Saying “I knew it!” afterward can’t.

After the test, the outcomeOutcomeWhat actually happens in a trial. tells you whether your prediction was right, partly right, or wrong. ALL THREE are useful! A “wrong” prediction does not mean the test failed. It often teaches you the most, because it shows you something you didn’t know yet.

Part 2 · The Predict–Test–Compare Cycle

Scientists use three steps over and over. Engineers use them to test bridges, doctors use them to test medicines, and you can use them to test marble crashes:

  • PREDICT: Write down what you think will happen and why. Use what you already know.
  • TEST: Run several trialsTrialOne run of a test.. Change only ONE variableVariableSomething that can change. at a time. Record each outcome.
  • COMPARE: Look at your predictions next to your outcomes. Did they match? Spot any patternPatternSomething that repeats. across the trials.

One trial alone can fool you. Three trials can start to show a pattern, and more trials make you surer. Some real studies run THOUSANDS of trials.

Part 3 · Pull, Predict, Let Go

Remember the Newton’s cradle from the Warm-Up? Now test it. Pull back 1, 2, then 3 balls. Predict first, then run 3 trials of each.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

In an investigation, what's the difference between a PREDICTION and an OUTCOME?

AThey're the same thing
BPredictions are right; outcomes are wrong
CA prediction is what you THINK will happen; an outcome is what ACTUALLY happens
🎯 Right! Predictions come BEFORE the test. Outcomes are what really happens.
A prediction is your best guess BEFORE the test. An outcome is what actually happens during the test. Comparing the two is how investigations turn into evidence.
Q2

Why do scientists run MULTIPLE trials instead of just one?

AMultiple trials let them spot a PATTERN — and reveal when one trial was unusual
BTo make the experiment take longer
CBecause the first try is always wrong
🔂 Right! One trial could be a fluke. Multiple trials reveal real patterns.
Multiple trials aren't about making things take longer — they help spot PATTERNS that one trial can't show. Sometimes a single trial gives a lucky or unusual result.
Q3

You predict that bigger marbles will push targets farther. After running 5 trials, the SMALLER marble pushed the target farther. What should you do?

AIgnore the results — your prediction said bigger
BUpdate your understanding — the evidence shows something different than you predicted
CThrow out the whole test
🔍 Right! Updating your thinking based on real evidence is exactly what science is. A "wrong" prediction taught you something.
When the evidence shows something different than your prediction, that's exactly when you LEARN. The right move is to update your understanding — not ignore the data or scrap the test.
