📘 The Lesson: What Makes a Good Model
Drawings that explain, not just label
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · A Model Explains, Not Just Names

You can draw a Florida bird and label its beak. That's useful. But it's not yet a modelModelA way to show how something works.. A model goes one step further: it explains how the part helps. It tells your reader why the part matters.

Here's the difference:

  • Just a label: "This is a beak." (Names a structure.)
  • A model: "This long, thin, curved beak → reaches deep into a flower → to sip nectar → so the bird can eat." (Explains the structure's job.)
🛑
Stop & Think
"You see a drawing of a turtle with the word shell next to its shell. Is that a model? Why or why not?"

Part 2 · Three Things Every Good Model Has

When you build a model of a Florida organism's structure, three things make it strong:

  • Labels. Name the structure clearly.
  • Arrows that show action. An arrow goes from the structure to what it does.
  • A short caption. One sentence on the side: "This structure helps the animal meet its survival need by ___."

Scientists use many kinds of models: a drawing on paper, a clay build, a computer model, or even a clear explanation you say out loud. Each kind is good for a different job.

A mix-up to watch for: A pretty drawing is not the same as a strong model. A stick figure with good labels and arrows can explain more than a beautiful painting with no words. A model's job is to explain, not to look fancy.

🧩 Explanatory Model: How an Anhinga Catches Fish
🖼️
Suggested Image — Generate This
A wide, kid-friendly annotated diagram of an anhinga (long-necked black Florida waterbird) shown underwater chasing a fish. The whole thing should look like an "explanatory model" — not just a labeled picture. Show three arrows pointing FROM each structure TO what it does: (1) from the long sharp beak → to a fish, with the words "SPEARS FISH"; (2) from the sleek streamlined body → to a curved arrow showing how the body moves through water, with the words "CUTS THROUGH WATER"; (3) from the dark feathers → to small bubbles in the water, with the words "LETS WATER IN SO THE BIRD CAN STAY SUBMERGED." Underneath the bird, include a short caption: "Each structure helps the anhinga meet its SURVIVAL NEED for food." Cream/transparent background. Optima palette: navy, coral, amber, forest green. 16:9 ratio. Make all text clearly readable for a 4th grader.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

What makes a science model stronger than just a labeled drawing?

AIt uses fancier colors
BIt is bigger
CIt uses arrows and short captions to show how parts work together
🧩 Right! A model explains a relationship — that's what arrows and short captions do.
Colors and size don't make a model strong. The arrows and captions that show what each part does are what turn a drawing into a model.
Q2

A green tree frog's color matches the leaves where it lives. The frog's color is best described as a(n)…

ASurvival need
BAdaptation
CInternal structure
🐸 Right! The frog's color is camouflage — a kind of adaptation that helps it survive in its habitat.
A survival need is what an organism must have (food, water, etc.). The frog's green color is a trait that helps it survive — that's called an adaptation.
Q3

Which of these counts as a science model per NGSS?

AA drawing, a 3-D build, or a clear out-loud explanation of how a structure works
BOnly a computer model
COnly a very detailed art drawing
🧩 Right! Models can be 2-D, 3-D, mental, or computer — what counts is whether it explains the relationship.
All four kinds count: drawings, 3-D builds, clear explanations, and computer models. The key is whether the model explains a relationship — not how fancy it is.
