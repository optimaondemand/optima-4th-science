📘 The Lesson: Two Kinds of Resources
Will it come back, or is it gone for good?
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · What Counts as a Resource?

A resourceResourceSomething from Earth that people use. is anything from Earth that people use to live and work. Water for drinking, sunlight for solar panels, wood for building, oil for fuel, sand for glass, copper for wires — all resources. Florida has a lot of them.

Scientists split resources into two groups based on one big question: how fast does it come back?

🛑
Stop & Think
"You drink a glass of water. Tomorrow, can you drink another one? You burn a chunk of coal. Tomorrow, is there a new chunk of coal in its place? What's the difference?"

Part 2 · Renewable vs. Nonrenewable

RenewableRenewableA resource that comes back quickly. resources come back fast enough that people can keep using them. NonrenewableNonrenewableA resource that takes millions of years to form. resources take so long to form that once we use them, they're essentially gone.

  • Renewable Florida examples: sunlight (for solar panels), wind (for turbines), water from rain and springs, trees that can be replanted.
  • Nonrenewable Florida examples: phosphate (mined for fertilizer), oil and natural gas, limestone (used for cement and roads).

Every resource has an impactImpactAn effect, especially on the environment. — a way it changes the environment when humans use it. Solar panels need land. Wind turbines can affect birds. Oil drilling can leak. Limestone mining changes the land. Even renewable resources have impacts — scientists work to keep them low.

Common misconception: Some people think "renewable" means "no impact." That's not quite right. Renewable resources come back, but using them still affects the environment. The real question is: how big is the impact, and how can we reduce it?

Good scientists check at least two sourcesSourceWhere information comes from. before making a claim about a resource — one source might be missing something the other knows.

⭐ Key Concept

Renewable resources come back fast (sunlight, wind, water, trees). Nonrenewable ones take millions of years (oil, limestone, phosphate). Both kinds have environmental impacts that scientists measure and compare.

Amazing Florida Fact
It takes about 1 million years for nature to form a single barrel of oil — but the average car can burn through a barrel in about a week. That's why oil is nonrenewable in a real way.
🌴

Florida connection: Florida is the nation's largest producer of phosphate — a resource mined from the ground for fertilizer that helps grow food worldwide. It's nonrenewable, but Florida's phosphate deposits will last for many more decades if we mine carefully.

🔭 Go Deeper · Why is "renewable" tricky for fresh water?
Florida's fresh water comes from rain that refills underground aquifers — slowly. If we pump out water faster than rain can refill it, the resource starts running short. So water is technically renewable, but only if we use it at the right rate. That's why Florida has rules about how much water cities and farms can use.

🔬 Explore: Build a Resource Comparison
Use two sources of information
⏱ 8 min

Pick a Florida nonrenewable resource (oil, phosphate, or limestone) and a Florida renewable resource (solar, wind, or fresh water). By step 4 you'll have a comparison chart with evidence from two different sources.

1
Step 1
Pick your two resources

Draw a chart in your notebook with two columns. Label one column with the name of your renewable resource and the other with your nonrenewable resource.

2
Step 2
List what people use it for

Under each resource, write at least TWO things people in Florida use it for. (Use the lesson info OR ask a parent/teacher — that's your second source.)

3
Step 3
List the environmental impact

Under each resource's uses, write ONE environmental impact of using it. Don't just say "bad" — describe what happens (air pollution, land changes, water use, etc.).

4
Step 4
Make an evidence statement

Write one sentence comparing the two resources, starting with "Based on what I learned, ___ has a smaller environmental impact than ___ because…" Cite information from BOTH sources.

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
Florida's solar panels could power most of the state's electricity needs if we built enough of them — yet today, less than 5% of Florida's electricity comes from the sun. There's a lot of room to switch from nonrenewable to renewable.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"A friend says, 'My family uses solar panels, so we have zero environmental impact.' As a scientist, how would you respond? What sources would you check before agreeing or disagreeing?"
🔭 Go Deeper · Why do scientists always check two sources?
One source can be incomplete, outdated, or even wrong. Two sources help catch mistakes — and they often add up to a more complete picture. When you read about a resource on a news site, you might check a government data source too. The pattern across both is what counts as solid evidence.

Q1

Which Florida resource is RENEWABLE?

AOil pumped from underground
BSunlight
CLimestone mined from rock
☀️ Right! Sunlight is renewable — it comes back every day.
Oil and limestone take millions of years to form, so they're nonrenewable. Sunlight comes back every day — that's the definition of renewable.
Q2

Why are oil, coal, and natural gas called "nonrenewable"?

ABecause they're hard to find
BBecause they cost too much
CBecause they take millions of years to form, so they don't come back fast enough to replace what we use
🛢️ Right! Fossil fuels form over millions of years. Once we burn them, they're essentially gone — that's nonrenewable.
The reason is about TIME, not price or supply. Fossil fuels take millions of years to form. Once we use them, they're gone on any timescale that matters to humans.
Q3

A classmate says, "Solar panels have zero environmental impact." What's the most accurate response?

AAgree — solar panels are perfect
BDisagree — solar panels need land and resources to build, but their impact is much smaller than fossil fuels
CDisagree — solar panels actually cause more pollution than oil
☀️ Right! Renewable doesn't mean impact-free. Solar still uses land and resources to build — but the overall impact is much smaller than burning fossil fuels.
Renewable doesn't mean impact-free. Solar panels still use land and resources to build — but their environmental impact is much smaller than burning oil or coal, not larger.
