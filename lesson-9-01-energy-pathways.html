📘 The Lesson: Tracing Energy from the Sun
Where does the energy in every living thing actually start?
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · The Sun is the Starting Point

Every animal on Earth eats food. Food gives an animal the energyEnergyThe ability to do work or cause change. it needs to move, grow, and stay warm. But here's the part that surprises a lot of people: animals can't make their own food. Not a single one. Not a hawk, not a shark, not you.

So where does the energy come from? Follow the trail backward and it almost always leads to the same place: the Sun. Sunlight is the giant battery that nearly all life on Earth runs on.

🛑
Stop & Think
"If animals can't make their own food, how do you think a hawk in Florida ends up with energy from the Sun? Take a guess before you read on."

Part 2 · Plants Are the Middle Step

Plants do something amazing. They take in sunlight, pull water up from their roots, and breathe in a gas from the air. Then they use the sunlight's energy to put those pieces together and build their own food — sugar. We call this process photosynthesisPhotosynthesisHow plants use sunlight + water + air to make their own food..

Because plants make their own food, scientists call them producersProducerA living thing — usually a plant — that makes its own food using sunlight.. Producers are the doorway. They are the only living things on land that can grab energy directly from the Sun and turn it into something you can eat.

🌱 What Do Producers Need?

Producers can't make food from sunlight alone — they need four key inputs: sunlight (energy), water (pulled up through roots), carbon dioxide from the air (absorbed through tiny pores in their leaves), and minerals from the soil (like nitrogen and phosphorus that help them grow). Take away any one of these and the plant can't produce food — which means the entire food chain above it would collapse!

When a grasshopper eats a plant, the energy stored in that plant gets passed into the grasshopper. When a green tree frog eats the grasshopper, the energy gets passed again. The grasshopper and the frog are both consumersConsumerA living thing that gets its energy by eating other living things. — but the energy started in the Sun and traveled through the plant first.

Amazing Florida Fact
A single blade of sawgrass in the Everglades can grow more than 9 feet tall using only sunlight, water, and air — no soil food required.
🌴

Florida connection: The Everglades is one of the largest sunlight-powered food systems in North America. The sawgrass in those wetlands feeds insects, snails, and birds — which feed alligators, herons, and panthers. Every animal in that system is borrowing energy that started 93 million miles away in the Sun.

🔭 Go Deeper · What about the ocean?
In the ocean, the producers aren't usually big plants — they're tiny floating living things called phytoplankton. They are so small you can barely see them, but together they catch enough sunlight to feed nearly every animal in the sea, from a tiny shrimp all the way up to a humpback whale.

🔬 Explore: Tracing One Florida Food Chain
Step-by-step, follow the energy
⏱ 8 min

We're going to trace one Florida food chain — a wetland chain from the Everglades — step by step. After each step, sketch what you see in your notebook and label it. By the end, you'll have your own model.

1
Step 1
Start at the Sun

Sketch a bright sun in the top-left of your page. Above it write: "All the energy starts here." This is your starting point. Every arrow in your food chain will eventually trace back to this.

2
Step 2
Add the producer

Draw an arrow from the Sun to a tall blade of sawgrass. Label the sawgrass Producer. The arrow shows energy moving from the Sun into the plant. This is the only step where energy gets "captured" from sunlight — using photosynthesisPhotosynthesisPlants making food from sunlight + water + air..

3
Step 3
Add the first consumer

Draw an arrow from the sawgrass to a grasshopper. Label the grasshopper Consumer. When the grasshopper eats the plant, the energy that was stored in the plant moves into the grasshopper. The grasshopper didn't make energy — it borrowed it.

4
Step 4
Keep going up the chain

Add a green tree frog and draw an arrow from the grasshopper to the frog. Then add a red-shouldered hawk and an arrow from the frog to the hawk. Label both as Consumer. Every new arrow shows the same thing: energy moving from prey into predator.

🤯
Amazing Energy Fact
Only about 1 out of every 10 units of energy moves from one step of a food chain to the next. The rest gets used up keeping the animal alive — moving, breathing, staying warm. That's why food chains usually have only a few links.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"Use your finger to trace the energy path from the Sun all the way to the hawk. Now say it out loud: 'The energy started in the ___ , then it went into the ___ , then to the ___ , then to the ___ , and finally to the ___ .'"
🔭 Go Deeper · What about the decomposers?
When the hawk dies, it doesn't disappear. Tiny living things — fungi and bacteria — break the body down. That returns nutrients to the soil, which helps new sawgrass grow. So decomposers don't really sit at the end of the chain — they connect the end back to the beginning. The chain becomes a circle.

AIt lives in the water
BIt gets its energy by eating another living thing
CIt makes its own food from sunlight
🐢 Right! Any animal that has to eat something else to get energy is a consumer — even ones that only eat plants.
Close — but the key isn't where it lives or whether it makes food. A consumer is any living thing that gets energy by eating another living thing.
