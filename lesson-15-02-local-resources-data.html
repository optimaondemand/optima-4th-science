📘 The Lesson: Reading a Resource Map
How patterns on a map turn into evidence
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · Maps Are Data

A map is more than a picture of a place. A resource map shows where things are concentrated using colors, dots, or shading. To read one well, you start with the map keyMap keyExplains what symbols and colors mean. — the little box that explains what every symbol means.

When you look at the whole map, you're really asking one question: where are things concentrated, and where are they sparse? That's the distributionDistributionHow something is spread across a place.. The places where a resource clusters tell you something about why it's there.

🛑
Stop & Think
"If you drew a map of Florida and marked every beach, would the dots be scattered everywhere — or clustered along the coast? What does that pattern tell you?"

Part 2 · Patterns Become Evidence

Scientists turn map patternsPatternSomething that repeats or clusters. into evidenceEvidenceInformation that supports a claim. by writing statements like this:

  • "The map shows phosphate mines are clustered in central Florida, not on the coast. The pattern is evidence that phosphate forms in certain types of underground rock found in central Florida."
  • "The map shows citrus groves in a band across central Florida, away from the coldest northern areas and the wettest southern Everglades. The pattern is evidence that citrus trees need a specific mix of warmth and well-drained soil."

Every resource's locationLocationWhere it is in the world. has a reason — usually related to geology, climate, or access to transportation. The availabilityAvailabilityHow much is there and how easy it is to get to. of a resource determines who can use it and how.

Common misconception: Some people think a resource is "everywhere" just because it's common. Most resources have specific locations — Florida produces nearly all of the country's phosphate but very little iron. Maps make those patterns visible.

Amazing Florida Fact
Florida produces about 75% of the phosphate used in the United States. Nearly all of it comes from a small section of central Florida — a tiny piece of the state feeding farms across the whole country.
🌴

Florida connection: Florida's natural springs put out billions of gallons of fresh water every day — water that's been slowly filtering through limestone for years. The map of springs almost perfectly matches the map of where the limestone meets the surface.

🔭 Go Deeper · What is a map projection?
Earth is a ball, but maps are flat. Every flat map stretches some parts to make others fit. That's called a "projection." When you compare two maps, make sure they're using the same projection — otherwise distances and sizes can look different than they really are.

🔬 Explore: Map Detective
Read patterns and turn them into evidence
⏱ 8 min

Pick ONE Florida resource from the lesson (phosphate, citrus, springs, beaches, or limestone). By step 4 you'll have a short evidence statement explaining its location pattern.

1
Step 1
Read the map key

Look at the Florida resource map above. Find your resource's symbol in the map key. Don't guess — read it carefully.

2
Step 2
Describe the location

In your notebook, write where on the map your resource is found. Use specific words like "central," "coast," "north," "south," "panhandle," "Keys."

3
Step 3
Spot the pattern

Is your resource clustered in one area, spread out across the state, or along the coasts? Write the pattern in one sentence.

4
Step 4
Write your evidence statement

Use this frame: "The map shows that [resource] is found in [location]. The pattern is evidence that [climate/geology/access] explains why it's there." Fill in each bracket using what you saw on the map.

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
Florida's underground freshwater system, called the Floridan Aquifer, is one of the most productive aquifers in the entire world. It supplies water to about 10 million people across four states.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"A new map shows wind turbines clustered along Florida's northwest coast. What would you investigate to figure out WHY they're there and not somewhere else?"
🔭 Go Deeper · How do scientists make a resource map?
They start by collecting data — sometimes from satellite images, sometimes from drilled core samples, sometimes from surveys. Then they plot every data point on a base map of the state. Computer software helps show patterns. A finished map represents thousands or millions of pieces of evidence packed into one picture.

Q1

What do you read FIRST on a new resource map?

AThe map key
BThe title
CThe biggest symbol
🔑 Right! The map key tells you what every symbol means — without it, you're guessing.
The title is helpful, but the most important first step is the MAP KEY — it explains every symbol so you can read the map correctly.
Q2

The Florida resource map shows phosphate mines clustered in central Florida. Why?

ABecause the weather is best there
BBecause that's closest to the coast
CBecause the geology under central Florida is where phosphate rock formed
⛏️ Right! Phosphate is a nonrenewable resource that formed in ancient seabeds. The pattern on the map reflects the underground geology.
Weather and coast don't explain phosphate location. The pattern reflects where the phosphate-rich rock actually formed — central Florida's ancient seabeds.
Q3

A student writes: "The map shows beaches along all the Florida coasts. Why?" Which is the BEST evidence-based answer?

ABecause people built them there
BBeaches form where land meets ocean — so they show up wherever Florida has a coast
CBeaches are inland too — the map is wrong
🏖️ Right! The pattern matches the geography — beaches only form where land meets ocean.
The map is right — beaches only form where land meets ocean. That's why they appear along every coast in Florida and nowhere inland.
