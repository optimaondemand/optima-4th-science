About This Unit

This unit starts with tools. In week 18, your student learns that scientists pick a tool to fit the question: a hand lens or microscope for things too small to see, a telescope for things too far away. Every tool has a limitation, something it can’t do.

Then the unit turns to energy. Your student finds out that moving things carry energy (and faster things carry more), that energy can transfer from one place to another, that collisions hand energy from one object to another, and that devices convert energy from one form to another. The last weeks look at sound, wind and water power, and how heat flows from warm to cool.

By the end, your student should be able to pick the right tool and explain why, connect speed and energy with evidence from fair tests, and trace where energy goes in a collision or a device.

“Energy doesn’t disappear. It moves, or it changes form.”The big idea that runs through Unit 5

How a Week Works

Each week has two lessons on one topic. Often the first explains an idea and the second is an investigation or engineering challenge, where your student plans a fair test, runs trials or designs something. Each lesson follows the same path:

🌅 Warm-UpA quick start and the day’s six words
📘 The LessonReading, pictures and short games or activities
🧠 Self-Check3 quick questions, not graded
✍️ AssignmentShow what you know, then turn it in
  • ❓A Big Question at the top that the lesson answers by the end
  • ✏️Part B: short answers in complete sentences
  • 🔬Part C: a bigger task, like describing what three tools would show or planning a test
  • 🗣️Use Your Evidence: agree or disagree with a claim, with two pieces of evidence
  • 📬Turn-in checklist at the bottom, ending with submitting in Canvas
⚖️

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “You should always use the most powerful tool you can find,” or “When two cars crash, all the energy just disappears,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

In this unit, the best evidence often comes from a test: a measurement, or a pattern across several trials. A helpful question at home: “What did the test show?”

The Field Station: Meet Sam Park

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition with a mystery to solve. This unit’s scientist is Sam Park, an engineer (someone who designs and builds things to solve problems) who works in the same made-up town as Unit 4, Palmetto Springs. In 18.01, dead fish wash up on Palmetto Beach and swimmers are coughing. A lifeguard brings Sam a jar of the water. What is hiding in it, and which tool can find it? More of Sam’s cases arrive as each week of the unit is finished.

Short videos
As they’re recorded, quick videos from your student’s teacher walk through each part of the page.
Four clues
Each game your student finishes unlocks a clue. With all four, they crack the case.
Answers on the page
Everything is typed or dragged right on the page and gathered at the end. No separate notebook needed.

Sam and Palmetto Springs are made up, but the photos and science are real. Ask your student: “Did you crack the case? What were the clues?”

The Unit, Week by Week

Each week shows both lessons, the words your student will use, and two easy ways to keep the science going at home.

18Week
Every tool has limits, so pick the one that fits the question.Tools for seeing Field Station · new
Lesson 18.01Tech Tools: Microscopic Things“How do tools help us observe things that are too small to see clearly?”Meets Sam Park and solves Case 18, “The Red Water Sample”: dead fish wash up at Palmetto Beach and swimmers are coughing. Hears a beach health alert, turns a “zoom machine” from their eyes to a hand lens, a light microscope and an electron microscope (all real photos), sorts tiny things by the tool that can show them, picks the right tool for four lab requests, then focuses a virtual microscope, counts red tide cells and checks the count against the real chart Florida scientists use. Bonus: focuses a hand lens at 2x, 5x and 10x.Words: microscope, hand lens, magnify, limitation, observe, detail
Lesson 18.02Tech Tools: Telescopes & Large Things“How do tools help us observe things that are too FAR AWAY to see clearly?”Solves Case 18, part 2, “The Star Party Wish List”: four kids each wish to see something in the night sky, and the town can rent just one tool. Guesses how long starlight has been traveling, opens a model telescope wider to gather more light, aims binoculars and a telescope at the Moon, Saturn and a star cluster, watches Hubble’s 1990 launch from Florida on an old TV report, and holds Webb’s shutter open to find thousands of galaxies in an “empty” speck of sky. Then picks the darkest place in Florida for the star party.Words: telescope, distant, light-year, limitation, observe, image
Ask at dinner
After 18.01: “What was hiding in the water at Palmetto Beach, and which tool found it?” (Listen for: red tide, tiny living cells; a light microscope can count them alive, but an electron microscope needs dried samples.) After 18.02: “Why do scientists put some telescopes in space?” (Listen for: Earth’s air blurs and blocks some of the light.) “Is the strongest tool always the best?” (Listen for: no, the best tool fits the question.)
Try at home
Look at a leaf, a fingertip or a grain of sand with a magnifying glass (or a phone camera zoomed in). What detail shows up that your eyes missed? At night, count the stars you can see from under a porch light, then from a darker spot. Which place shows more? If you have binoculars, try the Moon.
19Week
The faster something moves, the more energy it carries.Speed and energy
Lesson 19.01Motion and Energy“How are an object’s speed and its energy connected?”Compares soft and hard hits, learns that every moving object carries energy, and builds a speed-and-energy chart.Words: energy, motion, speed, evidence, observe, kinetic
Lesson 19.02Investigation: Why Faster Objects Have More Energy“What evidence — from carefully measured trials — shows that faster objects carry more energy?”Plans a fair test with a ramp and a rolling ball, runs several trials and measures how far its push moves a target.Words: trial, distance, speed, time, controlled, measurement
Ask at dinner
After 19.01: “If two balls hit a block, which one pushes it farther?” (Listen for: the faster one, because it carries more energy.) After 19.02: “Why run more than one trial?” (Listen for: one try can be a fluke; several trials show a pattern.)
Try at home
Roll a ball slowly, then quickly, into an empty box or a stack of paper cups. Which roll moves it farther? Try each speed three times.
20Week
Energy can move from place to place, and you can prove it moved.Energy transfer
Lesson 20.01How Does Energy Move?“How can energy move from one place to another — and how do we know it actually moved?”Finds four ways energy travels: heat, light, sound and electricity, each with a source and a place it ends up.Words: transfer, heat, light, sound, electrical, source
Lesson 20.02Investigation: Observing Energy Moving“What evidence can YOU collect to show that energy moved between two places?”Sets up energy “stations” and collects observations and measurements as evidence.Words: transfer, station, receiver, evidence, measurement, source
Ask at dinner
After 20.01: “Name a way energy moves without anything touching.” (Listen for: sunlight, sound through the air, heat from a stove burner.) After 20.02: “How can you prove energy moved?” (Listen for: measure a change, like something getting warmer, louder or moving.)
Try at home
Go on an energy hunt in the kitchen: find one thing that gives off light, one that makes sound and one that gives off heat. Where does each one’s energy go?
21Week
When objects bump, energy moves from one to the other.Collisions
Lesson 21.01What Happens When Objects Collide?“What happens to the motion and energy when two objects bump into each other?”Looks at collisions as energy handoffs and the ways motion can change (speed up, slow down, change direction).Words: transfer, collision, motion change, prediction, before/after, source
Lesson 21.02Investigation: Predicting Collisions“How can YOU use multiple trials to test predictions about collisions and find a pattern?”Writes predictions first, then tests collisions in several trials and looks for a pattern.Words: trial, collision, outcome, prediction, pattern, variable
Ask at dinner
After 21.01: “When two cars bump, does the energy disappear?” (Listen for: no, it moves into the other car, sound and a little heat.) After 21.02: “Why predict before you test?” (Listen for: so you can check your thinking against what really happens.)
Try at home
Flick one coin across a table into another coin. Predict first: which way will the second coin go, and how far? Try it a few times.
22Week
Energy can change from one form into another.Energy conversion
Lesson 22.01Energy Conversion“How can energy change from one form into a totally different form?”Traces devices from input energy to output energy, like a toaster turning electrical energy into heat and light.Words: input energy, output energy, transformation, device, convert, useful energy
Lesson 22.02Engineering: Design a Device That Converts Energy“How can YOU design a working device that converts one form of energy into another?”Follows the engineering design cycle: criteria and constraints, a first prototype, a test and a redesign.Words: criteria, constraint, prototype, device, redesign, test
Ask at dinner
After 22.01: “Does a toaster make heat out of nothing?” (Listen for: no, it changes electrical energy into heat and light.) After 22.02: “Do engineers get it right on the first try?” (Listen for: no, they test and redesign.)
Try at home
Pick three things at home that plug in or use batteries. For each one, say what energy goes in and what comes out (a lamp: electrical in, light and heat out).
23Week
Sound is a vibration, and moving air and water can do work.Sound, wind and water
Lesson 23.01How Does Sound Happen?“What is sound, really — and why do high notes sound different from low ones?”Learns that sound starts with a vibration and travels as a wave, and that faster vibrations make higher sounds.Words: vibration, sound, wave, device, frequency, amplitude
Lesson 23.02Moving Air and Water Energy“How can moving air or water do WORK — turning a wheel, spinning a fan, or lighting up a town?”Sees how wind and moving water push blades and wheels, and tests a pinwheel.Words: wind energy, water energy, motion, device, renewable, observation
Ask at dinner
After 23.01: “What makes a sound high or low?” (Listen for: faster vibrations make higher sounds.) After 23.02: “How can wind make electricity?” (Listen for: it pushes the blades of a turbine, and the turning makes electricity.)
Try at home
Hum with your hand on your throat and feel the vibration. Then stretch a rubber band over an open box and pluck it, tighter and looser. Which makes the higher sound?
24Week
Heat flows from warm to cool, and some materials carry it faster.Heat and data
Lesson 24.01Energy Moves Between Objects“How does heat move between objects — and why does a metal spoon feel cold but a wooden spoon doesn’t?”Learns that heat flows from warmer to cooler objects, and sorts materials into conductors and insulators.Words: heat, conductor, insulator, device, transfer, temperature
Lesson 24.02Data Task: Energy Changes Form“How can we use evidence to show that energy CHANGED from one form to another?”Reads evidence cards and names each system’s starting and resulting energy.Words: starting energy, resulting energy, evidence, device, claim, energy change
Ask at dinner
After 24.01: “Why does a metal spoon feel colder than a wooden one?” (Listen for: metal is a conductor, so it carries heat out of your hand faster.) “Does cold flow into your hand from an ice cube?” (Listen for: no, heat flows out of your hand into the ice.) After 24.02: “Where did the energy in a flashlight start?” (Listen for: in the battery.)
Try at home
With an adult, put a metal spoon and a wooden spoon in a mug of warm (not hot) water. After a minute, touch each handle. Which one carried the heat?

Ten Words That Carry the Unit

MagnifyTo make something look bigger than it really is.
LimitationSomething a tool can’t do. Every tool has some.
EnergyThe ability to cause change, like making something move, warm up, light up or make a sound.
SpeedHow fast something moves. Faster objects carry more energy.
TrialOne run of a test. Scientists run several to find a pattern.
TransferWhen energy moves from one place or object to another.
CollisionWhen two objects bump into each other and energy moves between them.
ConvertTo change energy from one form into another, like electrical energy into light.
VibrationA quick back-and-forth motion. Every sound starts with one.
Conductor / insulatorA conductor carries heat easily (like metal). An insulator slows it down (like wood or foam).

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Short answers, a bigger Part C task, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 5 Assessment: Energy, Motion & Collisions (take it slow): pick the right tool for a job; use ramp data and a fair test to connect speed and energy; explain what happens in a collision; trace energy conversions, sound, and wind and water energy; sort conductors and insulators; and write a claim with evidence.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned in Lessons 18.01 to 24.02. Answers are typed and submitted in Canvas.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Weeks 19–21 · Hands-on tests
Several lessons ask your student to roll a ball down a ramp, bump objects together or run a simple station. A book propped on a few others, a ball or marble and a small box are all they need. Safe, small and on a table or floor works best.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What did the test show?”
  • When something moves, bumps or warms up at home, ask: “Where did the energy come from, and where did it go?”
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Encourage more than one try. Scientists run several trials before they trust a result.

Try to avoid

  • Wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
