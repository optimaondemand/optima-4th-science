Discover why scientists look beyond Earth for resources!

Part 1 — Why Look Beyond Earth?

For thousands of years, humans have used resourcesResourceA material or substance that people use — like metals, fuels, water, or minerals. from Earth — metals for technology, fuels for energy, and minerals for building. But as our population grows and our technology advances, we use more resources than ever before.

Think of it like a lunchbox. If you only packed a little food but kept eating more and more, you'd eventually run out. Earth is our "lunchbox" — and scientists want to make sure we never go hungry by finding new sources of the materials we need.

Part 2 — Three Targets for Future Exploration

Not all space exploration works the same way! Different destinations require different tools and approaches. Scientists are focused on three exciting targets:

Mars Mining
Robots and rovers drill for minerals and metals beneath Mars's dusty red surface.
Venus Exploration
Too hot to land! Drones and balloons float through Venus's thick atmosphere to collect samples.
Asteroid Mining
Robotic spacecraft harvest gold, platinum, and iron from rocky asteroids drifting in space.
🤔

"Look at the three exploration targets. Mars needs rovers, Venus needs drones, and asteroids might need robotic miners. Why do you think each place requires such a different tool? What makes each location unique?"

Real-World Connection: NASA's Perseverance rover is already on Mars collecting rock samples. SpaceX is developing the Starship rocket to carry humans there one day. And companies like Planetary Resources were founded specifically to mine asteroids — the future is already being planned!

🚀 STEM Careers That Make This Possible

All of this exploration depends on people working in STEM — Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math. Here are just a few of the careers that make space exploration and resource discovery happen:

Geologists study rocks and minerals to figure out which asteroids and planets have valuable resources. Aerospace engineers design the rockets and rovers that travel to space. Environmental scientists find ways to protect Earth's resources while we search for new ones. Computer scientists write the code that guides rovers across the surface of Mars. And astronauts are the brave explorers who will one day walk on Mars — just like the sailors who once crossed oceans!

Which of these careers sounds most interesting to you? There's a STEM job for every kind of curiosity!

Venus and Earth are almost twins in size! Mars is about half the size of Earth. Asteroids are tiny by comparison — but packed with precious metals.
Curious? Go Deeper — The Moon: Our First Stepping Stone

Before humans set foot on Mars or an asteroid, scientists plan to return to the Moon first! NASA's Artemis program is working toward sending astronauts back as early as the mid-2020s — this time to stay.

The Moon has a rare gas called Helium-3 embedded in its soil by the solar wind. Helium-3 could one day power a new type of nuclear fusion energy — with almost no radioactive waste. Just 25 tonnes of it could power the entire United States for a year!

The Moon also has water ice in permanently shadowed craters at its poles. Future explorers could melt this ice, split it into hydrogen and oxygen, and use those to make rocket fuel — right on the Moon. This would make the Moon a "gas station in space" for missions heading deeper into the solar system.

🛸 Mission Control: How a Resource Mission Works
Trace the journey of a space resource mission — step by step!
⏱ 8 min

Mission Control Briefing
Follow the four phases of a future space resource mission below.
Phase 1

Identify the Need: Scientists study which resourcesResourceA material or substance that people use — like metals, fuels, water, or minerals. are becoming scarce on Earth. They decide which space destination — Mars, Venus, or the asteroid belt — might hold those materials. Telescopes and satellites help them scout from a distance before any spacecraft is ever launched.

Phase 2

Send Robotic Scouts: Uncrewed probes and roversRoverA robotic vehicle designed to move across the surface of another planet, gathering information and samples. are launched first — they're our "eyes on the ground" (or on Mars!). For Venus, scientists might send drones into the atmosphereAtmosphereThe layer of gases that surrounds a planet. Earth's atmosphere gives us air to breathe; Venus's atmosphere is toxic! to analyze gases without ever landing on the dangerous surface.

Phase 3

Extract the Resource: Once scientists confirm a valuable deposit, robotic miningMiningThe process of digging or collecting valuable materials from the ground — or from space rocks! machines drill, scoop, or collect materials. On an asteroidAsteroidA rocky object, smaller than a planet, that orbits the Sun. Many are packed with valuable metals., a robotic spacecraft might attach itself and slowly chip away at the surface over months or years.

Phase 4

Use Responsibly: Resources extracted in space could be used in two ways — shipped back to Earth, or used in space to build spacecraft, fuel stations, or even future space habitats. Either way, scientists must explore carefully to protect the environments of other worlds, just like we try to protect Earth's environments today.

Mind-Blowing Fact
Mars's north polar ice cap contains enough water ice that if it all melted, it could flood the entire planet in a 35-meter deep ocean! Future explorers could melt that ice for drinking water, grow crops, and even split water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen to make rocket fuel — right on Mars!
Curious? Go Deeper — The Rules of Space: Who Owns It?

Here's an interesting question: if someone mines gold from an asteroid, who does it belong to? This is a real debate happening today!

In 1967, most countries in the world agreed to the Outer Space Treaty. It says that no single country can "own" a planet, the Moon, or any other space object. Space belongs to all of humanity — not just one nation.

But the treaty doesn't say much about resources extracted from space. In 2015, the United States passed a law allowing American companies to keep any resources they mine from asteroids. Other countries are now creating their own laws, too.

Scientists and world leaders are working to create international agreements so that the benefits of space resources are shared fairly — and so that space doesn't become a new place for conflict. It's one of the most important conversations happening in science and politics today!
