🔑 Teacher Answer Key

Suggested answers, scoring notes, and how to apply the common rubric.

Scoring approach. Each question shows ONE possible strong answer. Student answers may use different wording but should hit the same key ideas. Use the score guide under each question to decide level: 0 = blank/incorrect, 1 = partial, 2 = full.

Part A · Hazard Cause & Effect

Q1

Land, ecosystem, people effects.

Sample answer (storm surge row)
Land: The beach eroded and salt water flooded the soil two blocks inland. Ecosystem: Sea turtle nests were destroyed AND a freshwater pond was contaminated with salt, hurting fish and plants. People: 50 homes were flooded and the town lost power for 5 days.
ScoringProficient: names a clear effect for each of the 3 areas with table evidence. 3 pts.
Q2

Friend says only people matter.

Sample answer
My friend is wrong because the table shows sea turtle nests were destroyed, mangrove habitats were damaged, and birds lost nesting trees. The ecosystem suffered just as much as people. And people depend on healthy ecosystems for clean water and fish.
ScoringProficient: cites at least 2 specific ecosystem effects from the table.
Q3

What caused the sinkhole.

Sample answer
A sinkhole forms when underground limestone dissolves over time, leaving empty spaces beneath the ground. The 20 inches of rain probably soaked into the ground and washed away the loose soil holding everything up, making the ground above the empty space collapse.
ScoringProficient: connects rain + limestone + collapse. Advanced: mentions groundwater.
Q4

Criteria and constraints.

Sample answer
Criterion 1: Must block storm surge water from flooding homes. Criterion 2: Must last at least 20 years without major repair. Constraint: Town budget is under $4 million.
Scoring2 criteria + 1 clear constraint. Constraint must be a LIMIT, not a goal.
Q5

Compare A and B.

Sample answer
Solution A is better at strong, long-lasting protection — concrete is tough. Solution B is better at helping the ecosystem because mangroves and oysters become wildlife habitat AND it costs much less.
Scoring1pt for each accurate comparison.
Q6

Recommendation.

Accept either
Either solution can be the answer — what matters is using EVIDENCE. Example: "I recommend Solution B because it costs $4 million less, helps wildlife, and the town has a budget constraint of under $4 million. The maintenance is medium but worth it for the ecosystem benefits."
ScoringProficient: ties recommendation to specific criteria/constraints with table evidence.
Q7

Revision.

Sample answer
I would improve the living shoreline by ALSO building a small wall behind it for extra protection during the worst storms. This fixes the weakness that mangroves alone might not stop a really big storm surge.
ScoringProficient: revision addresses a real weakness from the comparison table.
CER · Claim
Sample
A good engineering solution for a natural hazard balances safety, cost, and care for the environment.
CER · Evidence
Sample
Evidence 1: Solution A (sea wall) is super safe but expensive and harms wildlife. Evidence 2: Solution B (living shoreline) costs less and helps the ecosystem, but offers less protection in huge storms.
CER · Reasoning
Sample
Both examples show that NO single solution is best for everything. Real engineering finds the option that meets the most important criteria within the constraints — and often combines ideas to get the strengths of both.
Q9 · Reflection
Open-ended
Accept any honest reflection. Look for awareness of tradeoffs ("hard part: deciding when to use natural vs built solutions") and value ("rewarding: knowing my work keeps people and animals safe").

Common misconceptions to watch for:
  • Thinking engineers can 'stop' nature — clarify mitigation is reducing harm, not stopping the event.
  • Confusing criteria (goals) with constraints (limits) — give clear examples.
  • Picking the 'biggest' solution as the best — emphasize that the best fits the criteria and constraints.
