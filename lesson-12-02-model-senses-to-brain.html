📘 The Lesson: Building Pathways You Can See
Two animals, two models, one big idea
⏱ 15 min

Part 1 · What Makes a Strong Pathway Model

You've already met the four steps. A good modelModelA drawing, build, or explanation of how something works. shows them in order, with arrows that explain what happens at each step. The strongest models include:

  • An arrow from the stimulus into the sense organ — showing what the animal noticed.
  • An arrow from the sense organ to the brain — labeled "signal" to show information traveling.
  • An arrow from the brain to the body — showing what the brain decided.
  • A short caption tying the response to a survival need (food, safety, shelter, mates).
🛑
Stop & Think
"If a model only labels the four parts but has no arrows, is it still a pathway model? Why or why not?"

Part 2 · Two Animals, Two Pathways

The four steps stay the same, but the details change a lot between animals. Two Florida examples that look totally different — but follow the same pathway:

  • Bald eagle hunting: sees flash of fish (stimulus) → eye picks it up (sense organ) → brain decides "food, dive!" (processProcessWhat the brain does with a signal.) → eagle dives (response).
  • Florida bat hunting at night: sends out a high-pitched click → click bounces off a moth (stimulus) → bat's ears pick up the echo (sense organ) → brain calculates where the moth is (process) → bat swoops and grabs it (response).

Same four-step pathwayPathwayThe steps information follows from world to response.. Totally different ways of doing it. That's why scientists like to build multiple models — comparing them shows what's universal and what's special to each animal.

Common misconception: Some people think a model is "right" or "wrong." Most scientific models aren't either — they're good enough for the question you're asking. The bat model leaves out a million tiny details, but it's perfect for explaining how echolocation works.

Amazing Florida Fact
When a Florida bat sends out its clicks, it can hear back an echo as quiet as 1/10,000 of a whisper — and its brain processes that signal fast enough to dodge a moving moth. Same four steps, lightning fast.
🌴

Florida connection: Florida wildlife biologists use trail cameras and microphones to record stimulus-response pathways in the wild — like a panther stalking a deer or an owl catching a mouse. Each clip is real-world evidence they can model and study back at the lab.

🔭 Go Deeper · Can a pathway model be a 3-D build?
Yes! Some scientists use yarn, clay, or pipe cleaners to build 3-D pathway models. A piece of yarn might be the signal traveling from a sense organ to a clay brain, and a small arrow shows what the animal does next. Sometimes a hands-on model makes the relationship easier to see — and easier to teach.

🔬 Explore: Build Two Pathway Models
One animal at a time — then compare
⏱ 8 min

Pick TWO different Florida animals — anything you like. By step 4 you'll have two complete pathway models, side by side, ready to compare.

1
Step 1
Pick your two animals

Write the names of two different Florida animals at the top of your page. Try to pick ones that use different senses — for example, an owl (hearing) and a bear (smell).

2
Step 2
Build Model #1

For your first animal, draw four boxes labeled stimulus → sense organ → process → response. Fill in each box with a real moment that animal might experience. Use arrows between every step.

3
Step 3
Build Model #2

Now do the same thing for your second animal. Same four boxes. Different details in each.

4
Step 4
Compare

Underneath both models, write two sentences: (1) "These pathways are alike because…" (2) "These pathways are different because…" Use evidence from your boxes.

🤯
Amazing Florida Fact
A Florida mantis shrimp has 16 different kinds of color receptors in its eyes (humans have only 3). So when scientists build a model of how it sees, they have to use 16 different "sense" inputs — same four steps, much wilder details.
🧪
Think like a scientist
"A scientist builds a model of one Florida bird's pathway. Then they build a second model for a completely different bird. Why does building two help more than building one?"
🔭 Go Deeper · Why do scientists revise models?
A first model is almost never perfect. As scientists learn more — by watching, recording, and testing — they update their models. A bat's echolocation model from 50 years ago is very different from the model we use today. Each version is a step closer to how the world really works.

Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you!
Q1

What does a pathway model need that a labeled drawing alone does NOT?

ABright colors
BArrows showing how the signal moves between steps
CA long paragraph of text
➡️ Right! Arrows show the order and direction of the pathway — that's what makes it a model.
A labeled drawing names parts. A model explains the order of the pathway — and arrows are how it does that.
Q2

If you compare a bat's pathway model and a Florida black bear's pathway model, what stays the SAME?

AThe sense organ they use
BThe exact stimulus
CThe four steps of the pathway
🧩 Right! The steps are universal — stimulus, sense organ, process, response. The details change.
The sense organ and the stimulus are very different between animals. What stays the same is the four-step pathway itself.
Q3

A friend's model shows: stimulus → response, with no other steps. What is missing?

AThe sense organ AND the brain processing the signal
BNothing — the model is complete
CThe arrows
🧠 Right! A real pathway always includes a sense organ picking up the stimulus and the brain processing the signal in the middle.
An animal can't respond if there's nothing to pick up the stimulus and decide what it means. The sense organ and the brain processing the signal are both missing.
