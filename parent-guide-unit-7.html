About This Unit

The last unit of the year is about matter that changes. In week 29, your student learns the difference between a physical property (something you can see or measure without changing the material, like color or hardness) and the clues of a chemical change (a new color, bubbles, smoke, a new smell or rust). Then they use property evidence to identify mystery materials.

Week 30 sorts the changes themselves. A physical change, like melting ice or folding paper, changes shape, size or state, but it’s still the same stuff. A chemical change, like burning, rusting, cooking or rotting, makes a new substance that wasn’t there before.

By the end, your student should be able to describe a material by its properties, identify a mystery material with evidence, and explain whether a change made something new.

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Used all year

Every lesson ends with a short argument. A “classmate” says something like “Any color change means a chemical change happened,” and your student decides whether they agree, then proves it. Scientists call this CER:

ClaimWhat I think. One sentence.
EvidenceFacts from the lesson that show it. Two pieces.
ReasoningWhy that evidence proves the claim.

In this unit, the best evidence is a clue your student saw: a new color, bubbles, smoke, a new smell or a coating like rust. A helpful question at home: “Is it still the same stuff?”

The Field Station: Dr. Priya Nair

In Field Station lessons, science becomes a field expedition with a mystery to solve. The guide for the end of the year is Dr. Priya Nair, a materials scientist (someone who studies what things are made of and how they behave), at Palmetto Springs. In 29.01, it’s the end-of-year campout, and Ranger Bell’s Changes Bingo card lists eight things that happen around the campfire: which ones made a new substance? Your student pulls apart a toasted s’more, watches Lady Liberty turn green, and sorts changes on a conveyor belt to find out. In 29.02, camper Kai pans the creek the next morning and tags four finds GOLD!, SILVER, DIAMOND and an old COIN. Your student tests each one (a streak plate, a magnet, a scale) and proves what it really is. More cases arrive as each lesson of the unit is finished.

Your student finds out that looks can’t hold up a claim, prints a property “fingerprint” for a bolt (it’s steel, not silver: silver isn’t magnetic), knocks suspects off a board one test at a time (gray alone leaves five!), and runs a streak test on two nuggets: real gold leaves a gold-yellow streak, but Kai’s fool’s gold (pyrite) leaves a dark one. Ask at dinner
After 29.01: “When you toast a marshmallow, is it still the same stuff?” (Listen for: no, the brown crust is something new; but melted chocolate is still chocolate.) “Why is the Statue of Liberty green?” (Listen for: her copper reacted with air and rain and made a new green coat called patina. Nobody painted her.) After 29.02: “How could you tell real gold from fool’s gold?” (Listen for: a streak test, a scratch test, or how heavy it is. Looking isn’t enough.)
Try at home
Make toast, or look at a banana a few days in a row. What clues show that something new is forming? Then melt an ice cube in a cup. Is the water still water? After 29.02: hold a fridge magnet to a few spoons, keys and coins. Which ones stick? (Most coins and many spoons don’t: being metal isn’t enough.)

Eight Words That Carry the Unit

Physical propertySomething you can see or measure without changing the material, like color, hardness or mass.
Chemical propertyHow a material can change into something new, like whether it rusts or burns.
EvidenceSomething you observe that backs up a claim.
IdentifyTo figure out what something is, using evidence.
Physical changeA change in shape, size or state. It’s still the same stuff.
Chemical changeA change that makes a new substance, like burning, rusting, cooking or rotting.
New substanceA material that wasn’t there before the change.
ReversibleA change you can undo, like freezing water and melting it again.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Short answers, a bigger Part C task, and a short claim with evidence, submitted in Canvas. The Self-Check isn’t graded.
End of unit
Unit 7 Assessment: Properties & Changes of Matter (take it slow): identify mystery materials from their properties, sort physical and chemical changes, and write a claim with evidence about what makes a change chemical.

It isn’t a memory test. The questions ask your student to use what they learned in Lessons 29.01 to 30.02. Answers are typed and submitted in Canvas.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 29 · Things from home
Part C of 29.01 asks your student to find four things at home and sort them (a fridge, a rusty tool, a slice of toast, an old banana). Nothing needs to be cooked or burned: looking is enough. Part C of 29.02 asks a family member to secretly pick 3 small, safe things (a spoon, a cork, a key…) for your student to test with a magnet and a cup of water.
Safety
The lessons show burning, campfires and glow sticks, but your student never needs to light anything. The cabbage-juice Tool of the Week is a model on the page; if you try it for real, please help with the stove.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “How do you know?” or “What did the test show?”
  • When something changes, ask: “Is it still the same stuff, or did something new form?”
  • Use the “Ask at dinner” questions above. Talking first makes the writing easier.
  • Encourage a prediction before every test. A wrong prediction is still useful.

Try to avoid

  • Wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Looking up answers online. The questions ask for evidence from the lesson.
  • Skipping the submit step. Work isn’t turned in until it’s in Canvas.
