📘 The Lesson: Natural Resources Around Us
Discover where everyday materials come from!

Part 1 — What Are Natural Resources?

Natural resourcesNatural ResourceA material found in nature that people use — like water, soil, wood, sunlight, and minerals. are materials that come from the Earth and are used to meet our needs. They include things we can drink, build with, grow food in, and use for energy. Natural resources are essential because without them, our homes, food, and technology simply would not exist.

But here's a key question scientists always ask: Can this resource be replaced?

🤔
Stop & Think
"If you used up all the sunlight today, would there be more tomorrow? What about all the oil underground — if we used it all up, could Earth make more of it quickly?"

💡 Use evidence! What specific evidence from today's lesson shows why protecting local natural resources matters for the future?

That question is what separates renewable resourcesRenewable ResourceA resource that nature can replace in a short amount of time — like sunlight, wind, and water. from nonrenewable resourcesNonrenewable ResourceA resource that takes millions of years to form and can run out — like oil, coal, and most minerals.. Sunlight shows up every single day — it's renewable. But fossil fuelsFossil FuelFuel made from ancient plants and animals buried underground for hundreds of millions of years. like oil took 300 million years to form — once we use them, they're gone.

🔑 Key Concept

The same Earth that you walk on every day is hiding an incredible supply of resources — some near the surface (water, soil), some deep underground (mineralsMineralA natural, nonliving solid material found in the Earth's crust., fossil fuelsFossil FuelFuel made from ancient plants and animals buried underground for hundreds of millions of years.), and some right in the sky above us (sunlight, wind).

Some of these resources are renewable (nature keeps making them) and some are nonrenewable (they took millions of years to form and can run out).

Part 2 — Florida's Three Resource Categories

Scientists group natural resourcesNatural ResourceA material found in nature that people use. by what they are and how they're used. Here are three major categories found right here in Florida:

Water Resources
Rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers. Used for drinking, farming, and recreation. Renewable through the water cycle.
Mineral Resources
Phosphate, limestone, and silicon from Earth's crust. Used in fertilizers, construction, and technology. Nonrenewable.
Energy Resources
Sunlight and wind are renewable. Oil is a nonrenewable fossil fuel. All are used to power homes, vehicles, and industry.
🤔
Observe & Reason
"Look at the three resource categories. Phosphate and limestone are nonrenewable. Sunlight and water are renewable. What do you think makes one type renewable and another not? What's the key difference?"
🏖️

Real-World Connection: Florida has a mineral called silicon — found right in Florida's sandy soil! Silicon is used to make computer chips and solar panels. The very ground under Florida's beaches contains a material that helps power our technology.

This bar shows (roughly) how long each resource takes to form. Renewable resources refill quickly — nonrenewable resources took longer than dinosaurs have existed!
Curious? Go Deeper — Why Does Florida Have So Much Phosphate?

Millions of years ago, Florida was completely covered by a warm, shallow sea. Sea creatures — fish, sharks, corals, and tiny organisms — lived and died there for millions of years. Their bones, teeth, and shells sank to the bottom and were slowly compressed into sediment.

Over vast amounts of time, that sediment hardened into phosphate rock. When the sea level eventually fell, that ancient seafloor became the land of Florida we walk on today — loaded with phosphate just below the surface!

This is why Florida has so much phosphate and states like Colorado (which was never a sea) do not. Different places have different geology — and that means different natural resources.

🔬 Guided Exploration: From Earth to Your Community
Follow the journey a resource takes to reach your home!
⏱ 8 min

Natural resources don't magically appear in stores and homes. Let's trace the 4-step journey a resource takes — from deep in the Earth to your community. As you read, think about where you've seen each step in real life.

1

Natural Formation: Over thousands or millions of years, Earth's processes create the resource — rain fills rivers and aquifers, living organisms decompose into fossil fuelsFossil FuelFuel made from ancient plants and animals buried for hundreds of millions of years., minerals crystallize in rock layers, and sunlight pours down from the sky every single day.

2

Discovery & Extraction: People locate the resource and collect it. Miners dig up phosphateMineralA natural, nonliving solid found in the Earth's crust — like phosphate and limestone. from open pits, engineers drill wells to pump up water from the Floridan AquiferGroundwaterFresh water stored underground in rock and soil layers., and solar panels are installed on rooftops to capture sunlight.

3

Processing & Refinement: Raw materials are turned into useful products at factories or processing plants. Phosphate rock becomes fertilizer. LimestoneMineralA natural, nonliving solid found in the Earth's crust. is crushed and heated to make cement for roads and buildings. Oil is refined into gasoline and plastics.

4

Distribution & Use: Finished products travel by truck, ship, or pipeline to stores, farms, and homes. The fertilizer feeds crops at a farm in Central Florida. Electricity from solar panels powers a school in Miami. This is where local resources connect to your everyday life — and why conservationConservationUsing natural resources carefully so they don't run out. matters!

🗺️

What About Resources Florida Doesn't Have? Not every resource is found everywhere! Granite, for example, is a hard rock used for countertops and buildings — but granite is not found in Florida because of Florida's geological history. If you need granite here, it has to be brought in from Georgia, North Carolina, or even other countries. This is why communities trade with each other: no single place has everything it needs.

Think Like a Scientist
"We said communities trade because no place has everything it needs. Can you think of an object in your home that was probably made from a material that did NOT come from Florida? Where might it have come from?"
🤯
Mind-Blowing Fact
The Floridan Aquifer — the underground water system beneath Florida — is one of the most productive aquifer systems on the entire planet. It stretches under nearly all of Florida and parts of Georgia, South Carolina, and Alabama. The water in it fell as rain thousands of years ago!
Curious? Go Deeper — What Happens When Resources Run Out?

In the 1800s and 1900s, many people believed Earth's resources were endless. People cut down forests faster than they could regrow, pumped groundwater faster than rain could refill it, and burned fossil fuels without worrying about running low.

Today, scientists monitor resource levels carefully. In some parts of the world, aquifers are shrinking because more water is pumped out than nature puts back in. Phosphate deposits are also being used up, and scientists estimate we may run short within a few hundred years.

That's why conservation — using resources wisely and efficiently — is so important. There are three main ways people manage used resources: recycling (turning old materials into new products, like melting aluminum cans into new ones), reusing (finding a new purpose for something instead of throwing it away), and disposal (putting waste in a landfill or incinerator when it can't be recycled). Turning off the tap while brushing your teeth, recycling metal and plastic, and using solar energy instead of fossil fuels are all ways we can stretch nonrenewable resources further and protect renewable ones.
